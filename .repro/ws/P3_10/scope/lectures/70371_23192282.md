## Page 7

Case 1
A 45-year-old white man comes to the office for follow-up of type 2 diabetes. He has no acute
complaints. The patient has taken metformin since being diagnosed with diabetes 6 years ago, after
efforts at lifestyle change produced only slight improvements in blood sugar. He had some initial
gastrointestinal side-effects that improved with time, and otherwise tolerated the medication and dose
increases without problems. The patient lost several pounds with metformin and lifestyle changes but
has regained some of that weight in the years since. His blood glucose was initially well-controlled, but
for the past year his hemoglobin A1c has been above the therapeutic goal and the dose of metformin
was increased.
Along with type 2 diabetes, the patient has history of hyperlipidemia. His current medications are
metformin 1000 mg twice daily (last increased 3 months ago), pravastatin 10 mg daily, a multivitamin,
and occasional ibuprofen for musculoskeletal aches. He has family history of type 2 diabetes in his
mother and brother. He does not smoke. He is very apprehensive about taking “shots” or pills that
could damage his pancreas.
The patient is 175 cm (5’9”) tall and weighs 92 kg (202 lbs), with a body mass index of 30. His blood
pressure is 128/82, sensation in his extremities is intact, and there are no other abnormalities on
physical exam. Recent fasting blood tests showed glucose 140 mg/dL, hemoglobin A1c 8.3%, total
cholesterol 194 mg/dL, LDL 120, HDL 42, and triglycerides 160.

• Treatment- stop metformin, hydration and treat the cause

For treatment of hypoglycemia
• Level 1 Glucose <70 mg/dL and ≥54 mg/dL - oral glucose
• Level 2 Glucose <54 mg/dL
• Level 3 A severe event characterized by altered mental and/or
physical status requiring assistance for treatment of
hypoglycemia, irrespective of glucose level

Clinical/biological risk factors
Social, cultural, and economic risk factors
Major risk factors
• Recent (within the past 3–6 months) level 2 or 3 hypoglycemia
• Intensive insulin therapy*
• Impaired hypoglycemia awareness
• End-stage kidney disease
• Cognitive impairment or dementia
Major risk factors
• Food insecurity
• Low-income status§
• Homelessness
• Fasting for religious or cultural reasons
Other risk factors
• Multiple recent episodes of level 1 hypoglycemia
• Basal insulin therapy*
• Age ≥75 years†
• Female sex
• High glycemic variability‡
• Polypharmacy
• Cardiovascular disease
• Chronic kidney disease (eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m
2 or albuminuria)
• Neuropathy
• Retinopathy
• Major depressive disorder
Other risk factors
• Low health literacy
• Alcohol or substance use disorder

## Page 37

Wrap-up
• Pharmacotherapy for type 2 diabetes begins with metformin,
but doesn’t end there.
• After metformin, —safety, tolerability, efficacy, price, and
simplicity can all influence the choice.
• Safety and dosing can be affected by pharmacokinetic and
pharmacodynamic interactions, and by other medical problems.
• Patients with type 2 diabetes often have other medical issues
that call for attention and can affect treatment.
