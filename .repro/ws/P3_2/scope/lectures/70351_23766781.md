## Slide 11

- Scrotal Layers, Paired With Their Pathology
- Tunica vaginalis (from processus vaginalis) → hydrocele; patent processus → communicating hydrocele + indirect hernia
- Cremaster (internal oblique) → cremasteric reflex: stroke medial thigh
- Femoral branch carries afferents up
- Genital branch caries motor efferent back to cremaster → elevation of ipsilateral testis
- Reflex arc mediated by the genitofemoral nerve (L1–L2)
- Dartos + skin → thermoregulation; the plane of Fournier gangrene
- Reflex is absent in torsion: the single most useful acute-scrotum exam finding
  - Presence of reflex does not exclude torsion

Notes:
Tunica vaginalis / processus vaginalis → hydrocele & hernia. The tunica vaginalis is the peritoneal remnant left after the processus vaginalis (which the descending testis drags into the scrotum) normally obliterates; fluid trapped in a closed remnant is a simple (non-communicating) hydrocele, whereas a persistently patent processus allows peritoneal fluid down to give a communicating hydrocele and, if wide enough for bowel, an indirect inguinal hernia.
Cremaster (from internal oblique) → cremasteric reflex. Stroking the medial thigh sends afferents up the femoral branch of the genitofemoral nerve, and the genital branch carries the motor efferent back to the cremaster, elevating the ipsilateral testis — a reflex arc mediated by the genitofemoral nerve (L1–L2).
Dartos + skin → thermoregulation and the Fournier plane. The dartos is the scrotal smooth-muscle layer that contracts in cold to wrinkle the skin and reduce heat-losing surface area for testicular thermoregulation, and — as part of the continuous dartos–Colles'–Scarpa's superficial fascial space — it is the anatomical plane along which Fournier gangrene spreads.
Absent reflex in torsion — the key acute-scrotum sign. In testicular torsion the cremasteric reflex is characteristically lost; it is the single most sensitive exam finding (absent in up to 100% of torsion cases, odds ratio ~47.6), though a present reflex does not fully exclude torsion.

## Slide 17

- Stages of Spermatogenesis
- Spermatogonia: type A dark (reserve) · A pale (renewing) · B (committed)
- Meiosis I: primary spermatocyte → two secondary spermatocytes
- Meiosis II: → spermatids (haploid)
- Spermiogenesis (remodeling, no division): Golgi → acrosome; protamines replace histones; flagellum + mitochondrial midpiece; cytoplasm shed as residual body (Sertoli phagocytoses it)
- Spermiation: released into the lumen — immotile and infertile
- Motility is gained in the epididymis; capacitation and the acrosome reaction occur in the female tract — classic distractor

Notes:
Spermatogonia (mitotic pool, on the basement membrane). Type A-dark = reserve stem cells (rarely divide, the emergency backup), type A-pale = renewing/active stem cells (routine self-renewal + differentiation), and type B = committed cells that divide into primary spermatocytes and enter meiosis.
Meiosis I. One diploid primary spermatocyte → two haploid secondary spermatocytes; the long pachytene prophase (~3 weeks) is why primary spermatocytes are the most conspicuous germ cell on biopsy. [1][4]
Meiosis II. Each secondary spermatocyte divides quickly (<10 h, so rarely seen) into spermatids — haploid (1N).
Spermiogenesis (remodeling, NO cell division). Round spermatid → spermatozoon: the Golgi forms the acrosomal cap; protamines replace histones for extreme nuclear condensation and transcriptional silencing; a flagellum and mitochondrial midpiece are built; and excess cytoplasm is shed as a residual body that Sertoli cells phagocytose.
Spermiation. The spermatozoon is released into the tubular lumen but is still immotile and infertile at this point.
The classic distractors — the "where" questions. Motility is acquired later, during transit/storage in the epididymis; capacitation and the acrosome reaction occur in the female reproductive tract (capacitation begins in the uterus, hyperactivation/acrosome reaction in the oviduct/ampulla), NOT in the testis
the testis builds the sperm (through spermiogenesis), the epididymis makes it move, and the female tract makes it fertile (capacitation + acrosome reaction).

## Slide 18

- The Sertoli Cell — Most Testable Cell of the Day
- Forms the blood-testis barrier → immune privilege for antigenically novel post-meiotic cells
  - Explains antisperm antibodies after vasectomy or trauma, and the severity of mumps orchitis
- Responds to FSH
- Secretes androgen-binding protein, inhibin B (FSH feedback + spermatogenesis marker), AMH (fetal)
- Number fixed after puberty → sets the ceiling on sperm output
- Contrast: Leydig = LH → testosterone, androstenedione, DHEA (DHT is made peripherally, not by Leydig cells)

Notes:
Blood-testis barrier → immune privilege. Adjacent Sertoli cells form tight junctions that create the blood-testis barrier, sequestering antigenically "novel" post-meiotic germ cells (which arise after puberty, long after central immune tolerance is set) from the immune system.
Explains antisperm antibodies and mumps orchitis severity. Because those germ-cell antigens are hidden, any breach — vasectomy, trauma, torsion, or infection — exposes them and triggers antisperm antibodies; the same principle underlies the immune-mediated inflammatory damage of mumps orchitis.
Responds to FSH. Sertoli cells carry the FSH receptor, making them the primary target of FSH within the tubule.
Secretory products. FSH stimulation drives androgen-binding protein (concentrates intratubular testosterone), inhibin B (negative FSH feedback and a clinical marker of spermatogenesis), and AMH (fetal — drives Müllerian regression; falls at puberty).
Fixed number after puberty → sets the sperm-output ceiling. Each Sertoli cell supports a finite number of germ cells, so the post-pubertal Sertoli population caps maximum sperm production.
Contrast — Leydig cells (interstitium, LH-driven). LH → Leydig cells → testosterone, plus weaker androgens androstenedione and DHEA; note DHT is generated peripherally by 5α-reductase, not synthesized by Leydig cells
Sertoli = FSH → barrier, ABP, inhibin B, AMH, and the sperm-output ceiling; Leydig = LH → testosterone (DHT made peripherally)

## Slide 19

- Thermoregulation: Why the Testis Lives Outside
- Operates 2–4 °C below core temperature
- Pampiniform plexus countercurrent exchange cools arterial inflow
- Cremaster and dartos adjust position and surface area
- One mechanism, four diseases: cryptorchidism, varicocele, obesity, occupational heat all impair spermatogenesis through the same thermal pathway

Notes:
Operates 2–4 °C below core temperature because spermatogenesis (and DNA/enzyme integrity in meiosis) is heat-sensitive and fails at core body temperature.
Pampiniform plexus countercurrent exchange — the venous plexus wraps the testicular artery so cool venous return draws heat out of warm arterial inflow before it reaches the testis.
Cremaster and dartos adjust testicular position (closer/farther from the body) and scrotal surface area to fine-tune heat loss.
One mechanism, four diseases — cryptorchidism, varicocele, obesity, and occupational heat exposure all raise testicular temperature and impair spermatogenesis through this same thermal pathway.

## Slide 22

- The Hypothalamic–Pituitary–Gonadal Axis
- GnRH is pulsatile — and pulsatility is obligatory
  - Continuous GnRH exposure downregulates the receptor and shuts the axis off (hold that thought until leuprolide)
- LH → Leydig → testosterone (rate-limiting step: StAR cholesterol transport)
- FSH → Sertoli → androgen-binding protein + inhibin B
- Feedback: testosterone and estradiol centrally; inhibin B selectively suppresses FSH

Notes:
Plant the leuprolide flag now, collect it in pharmacology. Spoken endocrine handoff.
GnRH is pulsatile, and pulsatility is obligatory — hypothalamic GnRH must be secreted in discrete pulses (~every 60–120 min) to sustain gonadotropin output.
Continuous GnRH exposure shuts the axis off — sustained (non-pulsatile) stimulation downregulates and desensitizes the pituitary GnRH receptor, collapsing LH/FSH release (the basis of leuprolide and other GnRH agonists causing chemical castration).
LH → Leydig → testosterone — LH drives Leydig-cell steroidogenesis, whose rate-limiting step is StAR-mediated cholesterol transport into the mitochondrion.
FSH → Sertoli → ABP + inhibin B — FSH stimulates Sertoli cells to secrete androgen-binding protein (concentrates intratubular testosterone) and inhibin B.
Feedback — testosterone and estradiol exert central negative feedback (hypothalamus + pituitary), while inhibin B selectively suppresses FSH at the pituitary

## Slide 23

- Intratesticular Testosterone — The Concept Behind Case B
- Intratesticular testosterone ≈ 50–100× serum concentration — spermatogenesis requires the gradient
- Exogenous testosterone: serum T up → LH suppressed → intratesticular T collapses → spermatogenesis stops
- Testosterone is a male contraceptive
- Never start testosterone in a man who wants near-term fertility
- Every azoospermic young man gets asked directly about testosterone, “men’s clinic” injections, and anabolic steroids

## Slide 24

- Testosterone Transport: Why “Normal Total T” Can Mislead
- ≈44–65% bound to SHBG (tight, unavailable) · ≈33–50% to albumin (loose) · 2–3% free
- Bioavailable = free + albumin-bound
- SHBG falls: obesity, insulin resistance, T2DM, hypothyroidism
- SHBG rises: aging, hyperthyroidism, cirrhosis, estrogens, anticonvulsants
- The obese 45-year-old with a “normal” total T can still be biochemically hypogonadal
- → order free or bioavailable T only when SHBG is distorted

Notes:
Free testosterone measured by most labs is unreliable because the common direct analog immunoassay is inaccurate — it systematically misestimates free T and is not recommended; either calculated free T (from total T, SHBG, albumin) or equilibrium dialysis should be used instead.
The problem — the widely available direct analog radioimmunoassay for free T does not correlate well with the reference equilibrium-dialysis method, giving falsely low and unreliable values, especially when SHBG is abnormal.
Preferred alternatives — equilibrium dialysis (gold standard) or calculated free T using total T, SHBG, and albumin give far more accurate bioavailable/free estimates in the obese or SHBG-distorted patient.

## Slide 25

- One Hormone, Three Signals
- Testosterone → (5-alpha-reductase) → DHT
  - Type 2: prostate, seminal vesicles, genital skin
  - Type 1: skin, liver
  - DHT binds the androgen receptor with higher affinity, dissociates slowly, cannot be aromatized
- Testosterone → (aromatase, mostly adipose) → estradiol
  - Estradiol in men drives bone density, epiphyseal closure, libido, and central feedback
- Obesity → more aromatization → low-T, high-E2, low-LH pattern

Notes:
Testosterone → DHT via 5-alpha-reductase — peripheral conversion amplifies androgen signaling in target tissues.
Type 2 5-alpha-reductase — dominant in prostate, seminal vesicles, and genital skin (the isoenzyme blocked by finasteride/dutasteride for BPH).
Type 1 5-alpha-reductase — predominant in skin (sebaceous glands) and liver.
DHT is the more potent androgen — binds the androgen receptor with higher affinity, dissociates slowly, and cannot be aromatized, making it a pure, amplified androgen signal.
Testosterone → estradiol via aromatase (mostly adipose tissue) — a substantial fraction of male estradiol derives from peripheral aromatization of testosterone.
Estradiol in men — drives bone mineral density, epiphyseal plate closure, libido, and central negative feedback on the HPG axis.
Obesity → increased aromatization — expanded adipose mass converts more testosterone to estradiol, producing the classic low-testosterone, high-estradiol, low/inappropriately-normal-LH pattern of obesity-related hypogonadism.

## Slide 26

- Testosterone vs DHT: Division of Labor
- 5-alpha-reductase deficiency proves the split: normal internal ducts, undervirilized at birth, virilize at puberty — and no BPH, no balding. Nature ran the finasteride trial first.

| Testosterone | DHT |
| --- | --- |
| Wolffian ducts: epididymis, vas, seminal vesicles | External genitalia: penis, scrotum, urethra |
| Spermatogenesis | Prostate growth — and BPH |
| Muscle mass, bone, erythropoiesis | Male-pattern balding, sebaceous activity |
| Libido, voice deepening, growth spurt |  |

Notes:
Testosterone and DHT are the same androgen signal split into two jobs: testosterone builds the internal machinery (Wolffian ducts, sperm, muscle, bone, libido), while its more potent metabolite DHT builds the external and cosmetic male features (genitalia, prostate, hairline).
That's why blocking the conversion with a 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor shrinks the prostate and slows balding without stopping spermatogenesis — men born without the enzyme never develop BPH or male-pattern baldness.

## Slide 31

- Localizing the Lesion by Erection Type
- Reflexogenic erections preserved, psychogenic lost → suprasacral spinal cord lesion
- Nocturnal and morning erections preserved → the machinery works → strong argument for a psychogenic contribution
- Nocturnal erections lost + gradual decline → organic (vascular, neural, hormonal)
- Nocturnal erections are testosterone-dependent — one reason hypogonadal men lose them

Notes:
Sleep/morning erections test the intact neurovascular-hormonal machinery, so their preservation with lost psychogenic erections localizes to a suprasacral cord lesion (intact S2–S4 reflex arc, blocked cerebral input), whereas their gradual loss signals organic (vascular/neural/hormonal) disease—and because nocturnal erections are the most testosterone-dependent, hypogonadal men lose them first

## Slide 33

- Detumescence — and Why Some Men Fail PDE-5 Inhibitors
- PDE-5 hydrolyzes cGMP to 5′-GMP. (Say it slowly — the entire drug class follows from this line.)
- Sympathetic norepinephrine at alpha-1 + RhoA/Rho-kinase restores tone
- A scarred tunica (Peyronie) or fibrotic cavernosal muscle (diabetes, aging, post-priapism) cannot compress the venules → venous leak
- Venous leak fails PDE-5 inhibitors at any dose — the drug amplifies a signal the anatomy can no longer contain

Notes:
Second appearance of the tunica albuginea.

## Slide 34

- Emission and the Bladder Neck
- Sympathetic T10–L2 → coordinated contraction of vas, seminal vesicles, prostate
- → while the bladder neck closes simultaneously under alpha-1 tone
- Alpha-1a-selective blockers (tamsulosin, silodosin) impair both → retrograde or absent ejaculation
- Orgasm preserved, fluid absent — the #1 uncounseled reason men quietly stop tamsulosin

Notes:
Sympathetic T10–L2 fibers drive emission—coordinated contraction of the vas, seminal vesicles, and prostate—while simultaneously closing the bladder neck via alpha-1 tone to direct semen forward
So alpha-1a-selective blockers (tamsulosin, silodosin) abolish both, producing retrograde or absent ejaculation.

## Slide 35

- Ejaculation ≠ Orgasm
- Ejaculation: rhythmic pudendal-driven contraction of bulbospongiosus + ischiocavernosus
- Central serotonergic tone sets the ejaculatory threshold (5-HT2C inhibitory, 5-HT1A facilitatory) — hold for SSRIs
- Orgasm is a cortical event, dissociable from ejaculation:
  - After radical prostatectomy: orgasm without emission
  - On alpha-blockers: orgasm with retrograde flow
- Patients conflate the three — physicians must not

## Slide 91

- Reference R8 — Capacitation and the Acrosome Reaction
- Ejaculated sperm are mature but not yet fertilization-competent.
- Capacitation (female tract, hours): cholesterol efflux destabilizes the membrane; bicarbonate and calcium influx activate soluble adenylate cyclase → PKA signaling; tyrosine phosphorylation; hyperactivated motility.
- Acrosome reaction (at the zona pellucida): exocytosis of acrosomal enzymes (acrosin, hyaluronidase) enabling zona penetration; membrane fusion follows at the equatorial segment.
- Board distractor: neither event happens in the epididymis.

## Slide 92

- Reference R9 — Experiments of Nature
- 5-alpha-reductase type 2 deficiency (46,XY): normal Wolffian (internal) ducts; undervirilized external genitalia at birth; marked virilization at puberty from the testosterone surge; no BPH, no male-pattern balding in adulthood. The physiologic proof-of-concept for finasteride.
- Complete androgen insensitivity (46,XY): defective androgen receptor; testes present (often inguinal); AMH intact → no uterus; external phenotype female; presents with primary amenorrhea or inguinal masses.
- Together they dissect the axis: 5-AR deficiency separates testosterone’s jobs from DHT’s (what survives is testosterone; what fails is DHT), and CAIS shows both hormones act through one shared androgen receptor — the final common pathway.

## Slide 93

- Reference R10 — Worked Semen Analysis
- 34-year-old, 18 months infertility. SA #1: volume 0.8 mL · pH 6.6 · azoospermia · fructose negative. SA #2 confirms.
- Step 1 — volume and pH: low-volume, acidic, fructose-negative → the seminal vesicle contribution is missing → ejaculatory duct obstruction or CBAVD.
- Step 2 — exam: are the vasa palpable? Absent → CBAVD → CFTR testing for patient and partner.
- Step 3 — labs: normal testis volume + normal FSH fits obstruction (contrast: small testes + high FSH = non-obstructive).
- Step 4 — plan: surgical sperm retrieval + ICSI; genetic counseling.
- The pattern recognition, not the numbers, is the skill.

## Slide 94

- Reference R11 — CPPS and UPOINT
- Category III (CP/CPPS) is a phenotype-driven syndrome, frequently non-infectious — which is why serial antibiotics fail.
- Urinary — LUTS → alpha-blocker, behavioral
- Psychosocial — depression, catastrophizing → CBT, treatment of mood disease
- Organ-specific — prostatic tenderness → anti-inflammatories, phytotherapy
- Infection — documented → one targeted antibiotic course only
- Neurologic/systemic — IBS, fibromyalgia overlap → neuromodulators
- Tenderness — pelvic-floor myalgia → pelvic-floor physical therapy (often the highest-yield single intervention)
- Multimodal beats monotherapy; set expectations for management, not cure.

## Slide 95

- Reference R12 — Circumcision: The Evidence in Brief
- Protective associations:
  - Reduced UTI in infancy
  - Reduced HIV and some STI acquisition (strongest data from high-prevalence settings)
  - Reduced penile cancer (largely via phimosis/chronic inflammation)
  - Reduced HPV transmission.
- Risks: bleeding, infection, meatal stenosis, rare glans injury; anesthesia considerations.
- AAP position: benefits outweigh risks but are not large enough to recommend universally → parental shared decision-making.
- Medical indications in adults: pathologic phimosis, recurrent balanitis, BXO.
