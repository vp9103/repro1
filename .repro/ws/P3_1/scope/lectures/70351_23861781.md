## Slide 6: Question 1: Answer and Explanation

- A 23-year-old woman presents with mucopurulent vaginal discharge and post-coital bleeding. On pelvic exam, her cervix appears erythematous with scattered petechiae. NAAT confirms the presence of a protozoan.
- Which organism is the most likely cause of her symptoms?
- A. Chlamydia trachomatis B. Neisseria gonorrhoeae C. Trichomonas vaginalis D. Herpes simplex virus type 2 E. Ureaplasma urealyticum
- Trichomonas vaginalis is a flagellated protozoan that causes vaginitis.
- Classic findings include: Frothy, greenish discharge; strawberry cervix (punctate hemorrhages); post-coital bleeding; nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT is the most sensitive test for diagnosis. Motile trophozoites may be seen on wet mount (less sensitive, 44-68%).
- A. Chlamydia trachomatis: Obligate intracellular bacterium (not a protozoan); Often asymptomatic; may cause mucopurulent discharge and cervical inflammation; Cervix may show friability but not petechiae; NAAT is used for diagnosis, but it detects bacterial DNA, not protozoa
- B. Neisseria gonorrhoeae: Gram-negative diplococcus (bacterium); Purulent discharge, dysuria, cervical petechiae are not typical
- D. Herpes simplex virus type 2: DNA virus; Painful genital ulcers, systemic symptoms; Cervical Ulcerations, vesicles
- E. Ureaplasma urealyticum: Bacterium (lacks cell wall); Often asymptomatic; may be associated with urethritis or cervicitis

## Slide 7: Functional Histology of the Cervix

- LO: Correlate the histological structure of the cervix with its function.
- Cervix is lined by columnar epithelium (endocervix) + squamous epithelium (ectocervix).
- Provides a barrier and mucus secretion to regulate sperm entry and protect the upper genital tract.
- The ectocervix, like the vagina (i.e., the part of the cervix protruding into the vagina), is lined with squamous epithelium
- The epithelial lining of the endocervical canal is columnar mucous-secreting glandular epithelium
- This epithelium infolds to form the plicae palmatae ("palm folds"), also known as cervical crypts or glands.
- In the presence of high estrogen levels at ovulation, the cells lining the crypts secrete abundant alkaline secretions (grey arrows). This mucus lubricates the vagina and nourishes spermatozoa.
- Inflammation alters epithelial integrity → friability, bleeding, ↑ susceptibility to ascending infection, and progression to neoplasia (esp. HPV-related).

## Slide 8: Cervicitis: Definition, Types, Etiology

- LO: Discuss common pelvic infections, including those affecting the vulva, vagina, and cervix, and describe the pathogenesis of these diseases, common organisms involved, and related complications.
- Definition & Types:
- Inflammation of the ectocervix. Acute = mostly infectious. Chronic = often noninfectious (chemical, mechanical, autoimmune).
- Infectious (most common):
- Chlamydia trachomatis (most common, ~40%).
- Neisseria gonorrhoeae.
- Trichomonas vaginalis → classic “strawberry cervix” (punctate hemorrhages).
- HSV-2 → vesicles/ulcers.
- HPV (some types), Ureaplasma urealyticum.
- Associated with bacterial vaginosis.
- Noninfectious causes:
- Chemical: soaps, detergents, spermicides, douches, contraceptive creams.
- Mechanical: condoms, diaphragms, caps, tampons.
- Systemic inflammatory diseases: lichen planus, Behçet syndrome.

## Slide 9: Cervicitis: C/P, Diagnosis, Treatment

- Clinical Presentation:
- asymptomatic.
- Symptoms: mucopurulent discharge (leukorrhea), post-coital bleeding, dyspareunia, pelvic/abdominal pain.
- Exam findings:
- Yellow mucoid discharge from os.
- Swollen cervix with red punctate hemorrhages → “Strawberry cervix” (especially Trichomonas).
- HSV → vesicles/ulcers.
- Complications:
- linked with pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), infertility, ↑ HIV transmission risk.
- Diagnosis
- Nucleic Acid Amplification Test (NAAT) = gold standard (detects Chlamydia, Gonorrhea, Trichomonas).
- Samples: vaginal secretions or urine.
- Important to rule out co-infections with BV, Candida, or normal flora.
- Treatment
- Empiric antibiotics (covering chlamydia + gonorrhea) for high-risk patients or when pathogen not detected.
- Specific therapy based on pathogen once identified.
- Partner treatment (esp. trichomoniasis, gonorrhea, chlamydia).
- Infection with Trichomonas vaginalis may produce a “strawberry” appearance of the cervix because of focal round patches of dilated capillaries on the surface.

## Slide 11: Question 2: Answer and Explanation

- A 62-year-old woman with urinary incontinence develops intense vulvar pruritus after using antiseptic wipes. The exam reveals well-defined erythematous, weeping, and crusting papules/plaques on the vulva. No fever. What is the most likely cause?
- A. Allergic/contact irritant dermatitis B. Candida vulvitis C. HSV-2 infection D. Neisseria gonorrhoeae infection of vulvovaginal glands E. HPV 16 infection
- A. Allergic/contact irritant dermatitis: Common in older women due to thinner vulvar epithelium and reduced estrogen. Triggered by irritants (e.g., antiseptic wipes); Presents with erythema, pruritus, weeping plaques, and no systemic symptoms.
- B. Candida vulvitis: causes intense pruritus, white discharge, and satellite lesions. More common in diabetics or immunocompromised. Lesions are usually white and curdy, not weeping plaques.
- C. HSV-2 infection: painful vesicles or ulcers, often with fever or systemic symptoms. Lesions are not typically described as weeping plaques.
- D. Neisseria gonorrhoeae infection of vulvovaginal glands: Can cause Bartholin gland abscesses not diffuse vulvar plaques; presents with purulent discharge, not crusting plaques.
- E. HPV 16 infection----VIN and vulvar carcinoma. Lesions are white, thickened, or exophytic, not acute erythematous plaques. No pruritus or acute inflammation.

## Slide 15: Vaginitis: Etiology, Pathogenesis, Pathophysiology

- LO: Discuss common pelvic infections, including those affecting the vulva, vagina, and cervix, and describe the pathogenesis of these diseases, common organisms involved, and related complications.
- Etiology:
- Infectious causes: Bacterial vaginosis (BV): overgrowth of Gardnerella vaginalis & anaerobes after ↓ Lactobacillus.
- Vulvovaginal candidiasis (VVC): Candida albicans overgrowth triggered by antibiotics, hormones, immunosuppression.
- Trichomoniasis: Trichomonas vaginalis (protozoan STI).
- Noninfectious causes: atrophic vaginitis (low estrogen), allergic/chemical irritation, foreign body, desquamative inflammatory vaginitis, lichen planus.
- Pathogenesis:
- BV: Loss of protective H₂O₂-producing Lactobacillus → ↑ anaerobes (e.g., Gardnerella).
- VVC: Candida shifts from commensal yeast → pathogenic hyphae.
- Trichomoniasis: direct protozoal infection.
- Common mechanism: oxidative stress → ROS, cytokine release (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) → epithelial injury & inflammation.
- Pathophysiology & Clinical Features:
- BV: Thin, gray-white, fishy discharge; ↑ pH > 4.5; clue cells on microscopy.
- VVC: Thick, “cottage-cheese” discharge; erythema; yeast & pseudohyphae on KOH prep.
- Trichomoniasis: Frothy yellow-green discharge, strawberry cervix, motile flagellates on wet mount.
- Atrophic vaginitis: Postmenopausal; dryness, burning, dyspareunia; thin mucosa, loss of rugae, ↑ pH.
- Two epithelial cells are seen; the one on the left is 'coated' with gram variable bacilli (Gardnerella vaginalis) making the cell appear purplish. These are usually seen with some frequency in a patient experiencing bacterial vaginosis.

## Slide 16: Vaginitis: Complications, Diagnosis, Treatment

- LO: Discuss common pelvic infections, including those affecting the vulva, vagina, and cervix, and describe the pathogenesis of these diseases, common organisms involved, and related complications.
- Complications:
- ↑ Risk of pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), adverse pregnancy outcomes (preterm labor, low birth weight).
- Trichomoniasis increases susceptibility to HIV and other STIs.
- Chronic/recurrent infections affect quality of life.
- Diagnosis
- BV: Amsel’s criteria ( 3 of 4 are required: discharge, pH >4.5, clue cells, whiff test), Nugent score on Gram stain.
- VVC: KOH prep (budding yeast, pseudohyphae); culture for non-albicans species.
- Trichomoniasis: Wet mount (motile protozoa), NAAT.
- Atrophic: Clinical exam, microscopy (↑ WBCs, parabasal cells, ↓ Lactobacilli).
- Treatment
- BV: Metronidazole or clindamycin (oral or topical).
- VVC: Oral fluconazole (single dose) or topical azoles; longer regimens for complicated/recurrent cases.
- Trichomoniasis: Metronidazole/tinidazole (single dose); treat partners.
- Atrophic vaginitis: Topical vaginal estrogen; lubricants/moisturizers.

Notes:
The Amsel criteria are a set of clinical findings used to diagnose bacterial vaginosis (BV) at the bedside. A definitive clinical diagnosis requires the presence of at least three of the following four criteria:
The 4 Diagnostic Components
Discharge: Thin, white, gray, or yellow homogeneous vaginal discharge that smoothly coats the vaginal walls.
pH Level: An elevated vaginal fluid pH greater than 4.5 (normal vaginal pH is usually ≤ 4.5).
Whiff Test: Release of a strong, fishy amine odor immediately after adding a 10% potassium hydroxide (KOH) solution to the vaginal discharge sample.
Clue Cells: Demonstration of "clue cells" (vaginal epithelial cells heavily studded and coated with adherent bacteria) making up more than 20% of the epithelial cells visible under wet-mount microscopy.
Nugent Score (Gram stain): Quantitative assessment of vaginal flora based on Lactobacillus, Gardnerella/Bacteroides, and Mobiluncus morphotypes. Score 0–3 = normal, 4–6 = intermediate, 7–10 = bacterial vaginosis.
Amsel criteria = clinical diagnosis
Nugent score = Gram stain-based microbiologic diagnosis
Clue cells = hallmark microscopic finding of BV

## Slide 19: Bartholin’s Glands:

- LO: Correlate the histological structure of greater vestibular (Bartholin’s) glands and paraurethral glands with their functions.
- Location of the swelling: The mass is described as being at the posterolateral vestibule near the labia minora.
- Paired mucus-secreting glands located in the posterior vestibule at the 4 and 8 o'clock positions. Their ducts open into the vulvar vestibule and may be depicted near the 5 and 7 o'clock positions.
- The mucinous glands seen here make up the Bartholin's gland in the wall of the vagina.
- This small gland is about 1 cm in size and the secretions drain via a duct lined by cuboidal to transitional epithelium.
- Function and duct obstruction: Bartholin glands secrete mucus for vaginal lubrication, especially during sexual arousal.
- Their ducts open into the vestibule, and duct obstruction can lead to cyst formation or abscess, which presents as painful, fluctuant swelling.
- Clinical presentation: Unilateral pain and swelling with a fluctuant mass is typical of a Bartholin gland abscess.
- Paraurethral (Skene) glands; Female Prostate: infections are less common and usually present with urethral symptoms (e.g., dysuria, purulent discharge from the urethra), not posterolateral vestibular swelling.

## Slide 22: Question 5: Answer and Explanation

- A 68-year-old woman presents with chronic vulvar itching and pain during intercourse. On exam, there are white, thin plaques with areas of skin fragility and scarring. - A. Lichen sclerosus: a chronic inflammatory dermatosis, most common in postmenopausal women. Clinical and histological features are characteristic. Risk of progression to differentiated VIN and keratinizing squamous cell CA
- B. Squamous cell hyperplasia: thickened, leathery skin due to chronic irritation (lichen simplex chronicus); Histology: Hyperkeratosis, acanthosis, elongation of rete ridges; No dermal homogenization or band-like infiltrate; seen in younger women with chronic scratching.
- C. Vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia (VIN): a precancerous lesion associated with HPV (especially type 16); Histology: Atypical squamous cells confined to the epithelium; present as multifocal pigmented or erythematous lesions; no dermal collagen changes or lymphocytic infiltrate
- D. Vulvar carcinoma: a mass or ulcerated lesion; pain or bleeding; Invasive squamous cells
- E. Extramammary Paget disease: red, eczematous, or velvety plaques; Large pale-staining Paget cells within the epidermis.

## Slide 45: The squamocolumnar junction SCJ and the Transformation Zone

| LO: Correlate the histological structure of the cervix with its function. |
| --- |
| Predict functional outcomes that result from altered structure and function of the uterine cervix (e.g., cervical cancer). |

- Fetal stage
- The SCJ (where squamous epithelium of the ectocervix meets columnar epithelium of the endocervix) is located relatively low on the cervix.
- During development, the columnar epithelium extends outward.
- 2. Pre-pubertal stage
- The cervix is smaller, and the SCJ moves inward (up in the canal) compared to the fetal stage.
- This is because of cervical growth and changes in the hormonal environment (low estrogen state before puberty).
- 3. Post-adolescent (eversion and transformation zone)
- With puberty and estrogen stimulation, the cervix enlarges, and columnar epithelium everts outward onto the ectocervix.
- This exposed columnar epithelium undergoes squamous metaplasia, forming the transformation zone (high-risk site for HPV infection and cervical neoplasia). The area between the original SCJ and the newly formed SCJ is known as the transformation Zone (TZ).
- 4. Post-menopausal stage
- Due to low estrogen, the cervix shrinks, and the SCJ recedes further upward into the endocervical canal.
- The transformation zone becomes smaller and less accessible.

Notes:
These metaplastic changes occur throughout the reproductive period and until menopause.
The immature squamous cells in the TZ are the most susceptible to Human Papilloma Virus (HPV) oncogenesis, and these are the cells of origin of cervical carcinomas.

## Slide 50: Pathogenesis of Cervical Intraepithelial Neoplasia (CINs)

| LO: Discuss the common human papillomavirus types that affect the cervix and discuss the pathogenesis of cervical dysplasia and neoplasia, and cervical screening methods and prevention. |
| --- |

- CINs are the precursor lesions of cervical carcinomas.
- Arise from normal cervical epithelium; low-grade and high-grade CINs.
- The most important factor in the pathogenesis is the High-risk HPV (hrHPV).
- Other contributing factors (smoking, immune status)
- Low oncogenic risk HPV (types 6 and 11) and high oncogenic risk (types 16 and 18; longer to clear) based on their genotypes
- High-risk HPVs are the main pathogenic factors of squamous cell carcinomas of other tissues (e.g., oropharynx, tonsils, anus, vagina, vulva, and penis).
- Risk factors associated with HPV infections are early age at first intercourse, multiple sexual partners, and persistent infection with high-risk strains of HPV.
- The hrHPV infection into the cervical epithelium results in host genome alterations, leading to the silencing of various tumor-suppressor factors on one hand and inducing aberrant functioning of various tumor-promoting factors on the other
- The imbalance and instability caused by various hrHPV-derived oncogenic factors drive neoplastic progression

## Slide 51: Pathogenesis of Cervical Intraepithelial Neoplasia (CINs)-Cont

- The major oncoproteins are E5, E6, and E7 encoded by the HPV genome
- They disrupt the normal functioning of the major histocompatibility complex I (MHC class I), p53 and Rb, Notch1, Wnt, MAPK, mTOR, and STAT-associated pathways, which are central players controlling normal cellular growth, differentiation, and immune function
- HPVs induce carcinogenesis through dysregulation of the activity of tumor suppressor proteins such as p53 and RB by the viral E6 and E7 proteins, respectively---prevents the normal arrest of TZ cells in the G1 phase of the cell cycle.
- The viral E6 upregulates telomerase and binds to p53, leading to its destruction.
- E7 binds to the RB protein, leading to the release of the E2F transcription factors from the inhibitory effect of RB. This results in progression through the cell cycle.
- High-risk HPVs also integrate into the host cell genome. This integration results in increased expression of the viral E6 and E7 proteins, and host cell genomic instability----acquisition of prooncogenic mutations.
- Low-risk HPVs do not integrate into the host genome.
- The mechanism for low-grade dysplasia is accumulation of viral particles (DNA), producing the classic koilocyte
- Although CIN II/III has a higher risk for progression to invasive cervical carcinoma, not all women experience this; in fact, only about 20% progress.
- VIN (vaginal intraepithelial neoplasia) is similar in appearance to CIN but affects the vagina and affects an older population. It may represent a field effect from viral infection of the cervix.
