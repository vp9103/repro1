## Slide 7: Ovary: Structure and Function

- LO: Describe the microscopic organization of the ovary, uterine tube, and uterus
- Relate histological changes to the ovarian cycle, menstrual cycle, and pregnancy
- Microscopic organization:
- Cortex: Contains follicles at various stages of development, the corpus luteum, and corpora albicantia, embedded in a cellular stroma.
- Medulla: Loose connective tissue carrying blood vessels, lymphatics, and nerves.
- Tunica albuginea: Dense connective tissue layer beneath the surface epithelium.
- Surface epithelium: Simple cuboidal to squamous mesothelium (historically misnamed "germinal epithelium").
- Oogenesis:
- Begins in fetal life (~11–12 weeks' gestation); oogonia enter meiosis I and arrest as primary oocytes in prophase I until puberty.
- The LH surge resumes meiosis I, yielding a secondary oocyte plus first polar body; the secondary oocyte is released at ovulation and arrests in metaphase II.
- Meiosis II completes only if fertilization occurs, extruding the second polar body.

## Slide 8: Folliculogenesis:

- Primordial follicles: Primary oocyte surrounded by a single layer of flat (squamous) pregranulosa cells. These represent the largest pool of oocytes, formed in fetal life and held dormant until recruited or lost to atresia.
- Primary follicles: Cuboidal granulosa cells; zona pellucida forms.
- Secondary (preantral) follicles: Multiple granulosa layers; antrum begins to form; theca interna and externa differentiate.
- Tertiary (antral/Graafian) follicles: Large antrum; oocyte surrounded by corona radiata on the cumulus oophorus; ready for ovulation.

Notes:
The zona pellucida is a thick glycoprotein-rich extracellular layer that surrounds the oocyte (egg cell). It forms during follicular development and lies between the oocyte plasma membrane and the surrounding granulosa cells (corona radiata). Functions of the Zona Pellucida
1. Sperm Binding
The zona pellucida contains glycoproteins (ZP1, ZP2, ZP3, and ZP4) that allow sperm to recognize and bind to the oocyte. This is the first step in fertilization.
2. Triggers the Acrosome Reaction
After sperm binding, the zona pellucida induces the acrosome reaction, during which enzymes are released from the sperm head to digest a pathway through the zona pellucida.
3. Prevents Polyspermy
Once fertilization occurs, cortical granules from the oocyte modify the zona pellucida (the zona reaction), preventing additional sperm from entering the egg.
4. Protects the Early Embryo
The zona pellucida remains around the embryo during the first few cell divisions as it travels through the uterine tube. The blastocyst later "hatches" from the zona pellucida before implantation.

- A 30-year-old woman has had recurring pelvic pain with dysuria and dysmenorrhea for the past 5 years. On pelvic examination there are no abnormal findings. - Endometrial glands with stroma (correct): Endometriosis is histologically defined as ectopic endometrial glands and stroma, often accompanied by hemosiderin-laden macrophages.
- Enlarged, avascular chorionic villi: Describes hydatidiform mole/hydropic villi, a gestational trophoblastic process — unrelated.
- Neutrophilic abscesses: Suggest acute pyogenic infection (e.g., tubo-ovarian abscess), not endometriosis, which shows chronic inflammation and hemosiderin.
- Noncaseating granulomata: Point to sarcoidosis or a granulomatous salpingitis (e.g., foreign-body/Crohn), not endometriosis.
- Poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma: Endometriosis is benign; this describes malignancy.

Notes:
The presentation is classic endometriosis: chronic cyclic pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea, dysuria, and small (<3 mm) hyperintense peritoneal implants with adhesions in the cul-de-sac in a reproductive-age woman, improving on a combined oral contraceptive plus an aromatase inhibitor. Endometriotic lesions are estrogen-dependent, and aromatase inhibitors work by blocking local estrogen production within the lesions.

## Slide 14: Endometriosis

- LO: Explain how altered structure/function leads to clinical consequences (e.g., follicular cysts, tubal infertility, endometriosis, abnormal uterine bleeding, menopause).
- Endometriosis is defined by the presence of endometrial glands and stroma in a location outside the uterus
- Multifocal and involves pelvic structures (e.g., ovaries, pouch of Douglas, uterine ligaments, fallopian tubes)
- Less frequently, involves distant areas of the peritoneal cavity, periumbilical tissues, or laparotomy scars
- There are three types of endometriosis:
  - superficial peritoneal endometriosis
  - ovarian endometriosis
  - deep infiltrating endometriosis
- Risk of malignant transformation is mainly confined to deep infiltrating endometriosis
  - lesions that penetrate more than 5 mm beneath the peritoneal surface, often involving structures like the bowel, bladder, and uterosacral ligaments.
  - More prone to genetic mutations and cellular changes that may lead to malignancy

## Slide 15: Endometriosis-Pathogenesis

- LO: Explain how altered structure/function leads to clinical consequences (e.g., follicular cysts, tubal infertility, endometriosis, abnormal uterine bleeding, menopause).
- Pathogenesis
- 1. Origin from the uterine endometrium
- 2. origin from cells outside the uterus giving rise to endometrial tissue
- The leading theories are as follows:
- The regurgitation theory: endometrial tissue implants at ectopic sites via retrograde flow of menstrual endometrium through the opening of the fallopian tube
- The benign metastasis theory: endometrial tissue can “spread” to distant sites (e.g., bone, lung, and brain) via blood vessels and lymphatic channels
- The metaplastic theory: endometrium arises directly from coelomic epithelium (mesothelium of pelvis or abdomen), from which the müllerian ducts and ultimately the endometrium originate during embryonic development. Mesonephric remnants may undergo endometrial differentiation and give rise to ectopic endometrial tissue.
- The extrauterine stem/progenitor cell theory: stem/progenitor cells from the bone marrow differentiate into endometrial tissue.
- Implants have different characteristics than eutopic uterine endometrium.
- Endometriotic tissue has increased expression of proinflammatory and angiogenic mediators: prostaglandin E₂ (PGE₂), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). These promote inflammation, angiogenesis, invasion, and survival of ectopic endometrial tissue.
- Macrophages and other inflammatory cells recruited to lesions produce many inflammatory and angiogenic mediators.
- Endometriotic stromal cells express aromatase, leading to increased local conversion of androgens to estrogens and creating an estrogen-rich microenvironment that promotes lesion growth and persistence.

## Slide 16: Endometriosis-Morphology

- LO: Explain how altered structure/function leads to clinical consequences (e.g., follicular cysts, tubal infertility, endometriosis, abnormal uterine bleeding, menopause)
- Explain the pathophysiology and clinicopathologic features of functional disorders, including polycystic ovary syndrome, endometrial hyperplasia, adenomyosis, and endometriosis.
- Morphology
- Consists of functioning endometrium; undergoes cyclic bleeding
- Collection of blood in aberrant foci---appear red-brown nodules or implants, ranging in size from microscopic to 1 to 2 cm in diameter, that lie on or just under the affected serosal surface
- When lesions are widespread, organizing hemorrhage can cause extensive fibrous adhesions between fallopian tubes, ovaries, and other structures and obliterate the pouch of Douglas
- The ovaries may become markedly distorted by large cystic masses (3 to 5 cm in diameter) filled with brown fluid resulting from previous hemorrhage; chocolate cysts or endometriomas
- Diagnosis depends on finding both endometrial glands and stroma at sites external to the endometrium
- Endometrial stromal cells: hormone-responsive spindle mesenchymal cells; support the endometrium and differentiate into decidual cells during pregnancy.

- A. Ectopic endometrial tissue induces myometrial hypertrophy: This describes adenomyosis, where endometrial glands and stroma are located within the myometrium, causing reactive hypertrophy and resulting in abnormal uterine bleeding and dysmenorrhea

- C. Ovulatory dysfunction leading to anovulation: This describes dysfunctional uterine bleeding due to hormonal imbalance, not structural abnormalities like adenomyosis.
- D. Endometrial hyperplasia due to chronic inflammation: Chronic inflammation may lead to hyperplasia, but this is not the mechanism in adenomyosis.
- E. Submucosal fibroid disrupting endometrial architecture: While fibroids can cause abnormal bleeding, the histologic finding of endometrial tissue within the myometrium is specific to adenomyosis.

## Slide 19: Adenomyosis

- LO: Explain the pathophysiology and clinicopathologic features of functional disorders, including polycystic ovary syndrome, endometrial hyperplasia, adenomyosis, and endometriosis.
- Definition
- Adenomyosis is the presence of endometrial glands and stroma within the myometrium (uterine muscle wall)
- Pathophysiology
- Endometrial tissue becomes embedded deep within the myometrium between muscle bundles
- This ectopic tissue induces reactive hypertrophy of surrounding smooth muscle
- Results in a globular, enlarged uterus with a thickened uterine wall
- Clinical Features
- Menorrhagia (heavy menstrual bleeding)
- Dysmenorrhea (painful menstruation)
- Pelvic pain, especially pre-menstrual
- May coexist with endometriosis, but they are distinct entities.
- Gross and Imaging Findings
- Uterus appears enlarged and boggy
- Wall may be diffusely thickened
- Diagnosed via MRI or ultrasound, though definitive diagnosis may require histology

## Slide 22: Endometrial Hyperplasia

- LO: Explain the pathophysiology and clinicopathologic features of functional disorders, including polycystic ovary syndrome, endometrial hyperplasia, adenomyosis, and endometriosis
- Definition & Pathogenesis
- Endometrial hyperplasia is an abnormal proliferation of endometrial glands due to excess estrogen unopposed by progesterone
- It is a precursor lesion for Type I endometrial carcinoma (endometrioid type).
- Etiologies of Estrogen Excess
- Obesity (peripheral conversion of androgens to estrogens in adipose tissue)
- Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)
- Estrogen-producing ovarian tumors (e.g., granulosa cell tumors)
- Exogenous estrogen therapy without progestin
- Classification
- 1. Hyperplasia without atypia
- Increased gland-to-stroma ratio
- Glands may be cystically dilated
- Low risk of progression to carcinoma (1–3%)
- 2. Hyperplasia with atypia (also called Endometrial Intraepithelial Neoplasia, EIN)
- Complex glandular architecture
- Nuclear atypia: enlarged, vesicular nuclei, prominent nucleoli
- High risk of progression to carcinoma
- Associated with PTEN mutations

## Slide 24: Endometrial Carcinoma-Endometrioid

- LO: Differentiate between type I and type II endometrial carcinomas in terms of risk factors, molecular basis, morphology, and outcomes, and relate type I to hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal carcinoma (Lynch syndrome).
- Type I Endometrial Carcinoma (Endometrioid Type)
- Risk Factors:
- Mutations in mismatch repair genes and the tumor suppressor gene PTEN are early events in the stepwise development of endometrioid carcinoma.
- Estrogen excess: obesity, PCOS, estrogen-producing tumors, unopposed estrogen therapy
- Associated conditions: endometrial hyperplasia with atypia (EIN)
- Age group: perimenopausal women (peak 55–65 years)
- Molecular Basis:
- PTEN mutations (Cowden syndrome)
- Mismatch repair gene mutations (e.g., Lynch syndrome)
- Occasional TP53 mutations (late event)
- Morphology:
- Glandular architecture resembling proliferative endometrium
- Graded 1–3 based on differentiation:
  - Grade 1; A: well-differentiated glands
  - Grade 3; B: solid growth, marked atypia
- Clinical Features:
- Presents with postmenopausal bleeding
- Slow progression, often localized
- Good prognosis if detected early (90% 5-year survival in early stages)
- Type I (endometrioid) endometrial carcinoma may arise in patients with Lynch syndrome (HNPCC) due to inherited mismatch repair gene mutations → microsatellite instability (MSI).

## Slide 25: Endometrial Carcinoma-Serous

- LO: Differentiate between type I and type II endometrial carcinomas in terms of risk factors, molecular basis, morphology, and outcomes, and relate type I to hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal carcinoma (Lynch syndrome).
- Type II Endometrial Carcinoma (Serous Type):
- Risk Factors:
- Endometrial atrophy, not estrogen-related
- Older age: typically 65–70 years
- Not associated with obesity or hyperplasia
- Molecular Basis:
- TP53 mutations (early and central event)
- Serous tumors are preceded by serous endometrial intraepithelial carcinoma (SEIC), in which TP53 mutations are often detected, indicating a central role for altered p53 function in the development of this form of endometrial carcinoma, which shows significant morphologic and biologic overlap with ovarian serous carcinoma.
- Morphology:
- Papillary structures, marked cytologic atypia; C: High-grade by definition
- Diffuse p53 immunostaining due to mutant protein accumulation; D
- Clinical Features:
- Aggressive behavior
- Often presents at advanced stage
- Poor prognosis, dependent on staging

## Slide 44: Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS)-Pathophysiology

- LO: Explain the pathophysiology and clinicopathologic features of functional disorders, including polycystic ovary syndrome, endometrial hyperplasia, adenomyosis, and endometriosis.
- Pathophysiology: (↑LH, ↓ FSH, ↑ androstenedione, ↓estradiol, ↑estrone)
- 1. Underlying Neuroendocrine Dysregulation
  - The hypothalamic-pituitary axis in PCOS is intrinsically dysregulated, possibly due to genetic and environmental factors.
  - This leads to increased GnRH pulse frequency, which favors LH secretion over FSH; LH:FSH ratio (often >2:1)
- 2. Androgen Feedback Loop
  - LH stimulates theca cells → ovarian thecal overactivity--↑ androgen production (e.g., testosterone, androstenedione)--- ↓ FSH (negative feedback loop)
  - ↓ FSH → Impaired Granulosa Cell Function (↓ aromatase activity in granulosa cells); Androgens are not efficiently converted to estrogens → follicular arrest (first meiosis) and anovulation
  - This selective feedback maintains or further increases GnRH pulse frequency, reinforcing LH dominance.
- 3. Peripheral Conversion of Androgens to Estrogens
  - Androgens → estrone via aromatization in adipose tissue
  - Leads to chronic low-level estrogen without progesterone → endometrial hyperplasia or carcinoma
- 4. Insulin Resistance and Hyperinsulinemia
  - Insulin acts synergistically with LH to stimulate theca cells → more androgen production.
  - Insulin also reduces SHBG (Sex Hormone Binding Globulin) → ↑ free androgens, which perpetuate the feedback loop.
- 5. Absence of Progesterone Feedback
  - Due to anovulation, there is no corpus luteum → no progesterone---- menstrual irregularity and unopposed estrogen effects.
  - Progesterone normally slows GnRH pulses; its absence allows faster GnRH pulsate

## Slide 61: Ovarian Tumors: Endometrioid and Clear Cell

- In association with endometriosis

## Slide 69: Question 15: Answer and Explanation

- A 55-year-old woman presents with vaginal bleeding. Pelvic ultrasound reveals a unilateral ovarian mass. A photomicrograph of an endometrial biopsy is provided. Histologic examination of the ovarian mass reveals Call-Exner bodies and cells with coffee-bean nuclei.
- Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

- C. Serous cystadenocarcinoma
- D. Immature teratoma
- E. Dysgerminoma
- A. Granulosa cell tumor (correct): Call-Exner bodies + coffee-bean grooved nuclei + estrogen effect.
- B. Sertoli-Leydig cell tumor: Sex cord–stromal but typically androgen-secreting (virilization); shows tubules/Leydig cells with Reinke crystals, not Call-Exner bodies.
- C. Serous cystadenocarcinoma: Epithelial tumor with papillae/psammoma bodies, no Call-Exner bodies or nuclear grooves.
- D. Immature teratoma: Germ cell tumor with immature (usually neuroectodermal) tissue; occurs in young patients.
- E. Dysgerminoma: Germ cell tumor with uniform cells, fibrous septa, and lymphocytic infiltrate — no Call-Exner bodies.

Notes:
An ovarian mass with Call-Exner bodies (microfollicular structures with eosinophilic material) and cells with grooved "coffee-bean" nuclei is diagnostic of an adult granulosa cell tumor. These are estrogen-secreting sex cord–stromal tumors, explaining the postmenopausal bleeding and endometrial changes (hyperplasia/carcinoma) on biopsy

## Slide 70: Granulosa Cell Tumor

- Granulosa cell tumor (GCT) of the ovary belongs to the category of sex cord-stromal tumors that show differentiation towards granulosa cells of the ovarian follicle. There are two subtypes - adult GCT and juvenile GCT.
- Adult GCT, mostly seen in peri- and postmenopausal women, accounts for 95% of all GCTs and makes up 1-2% of all ovarian tumors--Postmenopausal bleeding, breast tenderness, pelvic mass
- Juvenile GCT, which occurs in children and younger women (<30 yrs), makes up the remainder 5% of all GCTs--precocious puberty, breast development and vaginal bleeding in toddlers
- Call-Exner bodies are to granulosa cell tumors what keratin pearls are to squamous cell carcinoma: they are a morphologic clue showing the tumor is attempting to recapitulate its tissue of origin (follicular differentiation.
- Small, bland, cuboidal to polygonal cells arranged in various patterns. Call-Exner bodies (Red Arrow): small follicle-like structures, filled with eosinophilic material. The cells may show nuclear grooves (coffee bean nuclei).
