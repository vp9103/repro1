## Slide 8

- Iodine Metabolism
- Iodine, which is required for thyroid hormone synthesis, enters the body in food or water and is reduced to iodide or iodate ion; Iodine supply can be limited in mountainous or inland (away from ocean) areas.
- Thyroid gland is the principal organ that takes up iodide and stores it

## Slide 9

- Thyroid Synthesis & Secretion
- Under TSH stimulation-Thyroglobin (TG) synthesized and extruded to follicular lumen
- Iodide transported into follicular cells (Trapping) and iodide oxidized by thyroid peroxidase (TPO) (Oxidation)
- TPO covalently links iodine with tyrosine on TG to form MIT and DIT
- Coupling of 2 DIT = T4 and 1 DIT + 1 MIT = T3
- Iodinated TG stored until stimulated; endocytosis and cleavage from TG following stimulation by TSH

## Slide 13: Transport

- In the blood, more than 99% of T3 and T4 are bound to proteins. The vast majority are bound to thyroid-binding globulin, or TBG. Hormones bound to TBG are not biologically active, and they cannot bind to their receptors.
- The small percentage of these hormones that are left unbound are called free T3 and free T4. These are the biologically active forms that can readily bind thyroid hormone receptors.
- Effects of Changes in TBG Levels.
- TBG levels are increased during pregnancy and with oral contraceptive use because estrogen promotes TBG synthesis in the liver. In these patients, bound and total thyroid hormones are elevated, whereas free T3 and free T4 remain normal.

## Slide 14

- Receptors are intracellular and binding results in non-genomic (cAMP) and genomic effects
- Non-Genomic: Stimulates Na+-K+ ATPase activity and mitochondrial enzymatic function. Rapid action
- Genomic: Increases βAR (contractility and heart rate; breakdown of energy from storage) and αAR integrin (decreases peripheral resistance) = increased cardiac output and increased BP
- Thyroid Functions- sequential and overlap
- Barreto-Chaves, et al., Endocrine Connections, 9: R59-69, 2020

Notes:
Nongenomic actions of thyroid hormone and steroids are in contrast initiated at binding sites on the plasma membrane or in cytoplasm or organelles and do not primarily require formation of intranuclear receptor protein-hormone complexes. Importantly, hormonal actions that begin nongenomically outside the nucleus often culminate in changes in nuclear transcriptional events that are regulated by both traditional intranuclear receptors as well as other nuclear transcription factors. In the case of thyroid hormone, the extranuclear receptor can be the classical “nuclear” thyroid receptor (TR), a TR isoform, or integrin αvβ3. In the case of steroid hormones, the membrane receptor is usually, but not always, the classical “nuclear” steroid receptor. This concept defines the paradigm of overlapping nongenomic and genomic hormone mechanisms of action.

## Slide 25: Hyperthyroidism

- 1. Metabolism:
- Increased Basal Metabolic Rate: Thyroid hormones increase the rate at which the body uses energy at rest, impacting oxygen consumption, respiration, and body temperature. --upregulate Na/ K ATPase and increase cellular oxygen consumption.
- Carbohydrate Metabolism: They stimulate the breakdown of glycogen and glucose production.
- Fat Metabolism: lipolysis
- Protein Metabolism:-- protein breakdown (catabolism)
- 2. Cardiovascular System:
- Heart Rate and Contractility: Thyroid hormones increase the heart rate and the force of heart muscle contractions,
- Cardiac Output: increased.
- Beta1 -adrenergic Receptor Expression: Thyroid hormones increase the number of beta-adrenergic receptors in the heart, making it more sensitive to adrenaline and other sympathetic nervous system signals
- Bone Cell Function: affect the function of chondrocytes, osteoblasts, and osteoclasts, which are cells involved in bone formation and remodeling.--Bone loss
- Digestive System: Thyroid hormones influence the speed at which food moves through the digestive tract.
- Reproductive System: through changes in prolactin / HPA axis – affect menstrual cycles, flow and fertility.
- Skeletal Muscle: catabolic.

## Slide 28: Radiological studies

- THE THYROID GLAND AND IODINE
- The cells in the thyroid gland take up and hold onto iodine. Iodine is an essential ingredient used by the thyroid cells to make thyroid hormones. Since thyroid cells use iodine, radioactive iodine can be used to both diagnose and treat thyroid problems.
- WHAT IS RADIOACTIVE IODINE (RAI)?
- Iodine can be made into two radioactive forms that are commonly used in patients with thyroid problems:
- I-123 (does not damage thyroid cells)
- I-131 (can be used to destroy thyroid cells)

## Slide 29: Radio iodine scan and uptake

- Definition: Radioactive Iodine Uptake, or RAIU, is a test of thyroid function. The test measures the amount of radioactive iodine (taken by mouth) that accumulates in the thyroid gland.
- How the test is performed: RAIU is a type of nuclear test that measures how much radioactive iodine is taken up by the thyroid gland in a given time period.
- Patient ingests radioactive iodine (I-123 or I-131) in liquid or capsule form. After 6 and 24 hours later, they return to have the radioactivity measured.
- A gamma probe is placed over the thyroid gland in the neck to measure the amount of radioactivity in the thyroid gland. This amount of radioactivity is compared with the original dose of radioactivity and reported as a percent of the original dose.

## Slide 39: Treatment

- Beta-blockers (beta adrenergic blocking agents like propranolol) decrease conversion of T4 to T3, which would slow heart rate and provide symptomatic relief.
- Anti-Thyroid Medications - Thioamides (Methimazole, Propylthyiouracil (PTU)); these interfere with the action of thyroid peroxidase, thereby inhibiting thyroid hormone production; Methimazole is preferred due to more favorable side effect profile.
- BOTH can cause blood count changes/ bone marrow suppression- Neutropenia, thrombocytopenia
- Liver – acute liver failure
- Pregnancy – PTU preferred drug in first trimester due to aplasia cutis in methimazole
- Radioactive iodine (RAI) ablation -RAI treatment (I-131 oral administration-- beta or gamma radiation) destroys the thyrocytes and can take up 3-6 months to have full effect; can’t be used if pregnant or have active eye disease; may result in long-term hypothyroidism and replacement of hormones with Levothyroxine.
- Thyroidectomy – in the case of large goiter, severe hyperthyroidism and intolerance to anti-thyroid drugs

## Slide 40: BETA BLOCKERS- Main stay

- β1-Adrenergic receptors have a stimulatory effect on cardiac myocytes, increasing contractility and heart rate. Thyroid hormones upregulate these receptors, leading to tachycardia and arrhythmias. For this reason, β-blockers are an important treatment for hyperthyroidism.
- Examples – propranolol , atenolol etc

## Slide 41

- Mechanism. Thioamides inhibit the enzymatic incorporation of iodine into thyroxine, a key step in hormone synthesis.
- PTU ( propyl thio uracil) and methimazole are actively transported into the thyroid, where they inhibit both the organification of iodine to tyrosine and the coupling of monoiodotyrosine (MIT) and diiodotyrosine (DIT) for synthesis of thyroid hormone; this works through inhibition of the enzyme thyroid peroxidase (TPO). In addition, PTU inhibits peripheral 5′-monodeiodinase, preventing T4 from converting to T3 in tissues.

## Slide 50: Symptoms

- Fatigue, muscle weakness – slowing relaxation phase of deep tendon reflex due to reduced muscle and nerve metabolism
- Cold intolerance/decreased sweating – due to decreased rate of metabolism
- Weight gain, despite normal or increased appetite and intake – Decreased synthesis of beta-adrenergic receptors, resulting in decreased sensitivity to catecholamines and subsequent reduction of glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, and lipolysis (oxidation of glycogen, glucose and fat).
- Impaired CV function – impaired ventricular contraction, bradycardia, and increased peripheral resistance, resulting in diminished cardiac output.
- Dry skin and hair loss
- Constipation – reduced gut motility
- Menstrual irregularities – impairs conversion of estrogen precursors to estrogens, resulting in altered FSH and LH secretion, Anovulatory cycles and infertility
- Depression

## Slide 54: Post partum Thyroiditis

- Thyroiditis is inflammation of the thyroid gland leading to damage of thyroid follicular cells.
- Course
- When these cells are damaged, premade (stored) thyroid hormone leaks out into the bloodstream. This unregulated release of thyroid hormone may initially cause symptoms of hyperthyroidism (high serum levels of triiodothyronine [T3] and thyroxine [T4]). Eventually, however, the damage can become so severe that the thyroid no longer produces T3 and T4. Once this happens, patients present instead with hypothyroidism (low thyroid hormone levels).

## Slide 56: Other Thyroiditis

- Subacute granulomatous thyroiditis (also called acute thyroiditis or de Quervain thyroiditis)
- Presentation
- This is the only type of thyroiditis to cause a painful goiter. Patients with subacute granulomatous thyroiditis usually present with a painful thyroid, neck, or jaw and symptoms of hyperthyroidism that generally resolve to the euthyroid state after several weeks.
- Riedel Thyroiditis
- Riedel thyroiditis, also called fibrosing thyroiditis, is a rare form of thyroiditis in which the thyroid gland, and sometimes other organs, become replaced by fibrous tissue.
- Presentation
- The rigid, fixed thyroid appears clinically as a hard, rock-like goiter, but there is no associated pain. Most patients are hypothyroid or euthyroid.
- Riedel thyroiditis frequently causes local compressive symptoms. It can impinge on the trachea and interfere with breathing. A unique characteristic of this type of thyroiditis is that it can affect structures beyond the thyroid. Fibrosis can spread to the parathyroid glands to cause hypoparathyroidism, or to the recurrent laryngeal nerve to cause hoarseness.

## Slide 67

- Papillary Thyroid Carcinoma
- Papillary thyroid carcinoma is the most common form of thyroid cancer, constituting 70% to 85% of all thyroid cancer diagnoses. It usually spreads locally to the cervical lymph nodes AND HAS A GOOD PROGNOSIS
- Risk Factors and Pathogenesis. Patients who experienced childhood radiation or who have a family history of thyroid cancer are at an increased risk for papillary carcinoma.
- chromosomal recombination, leading to gain-of-function mutations in the BRAF or RET oncogenes.

## Slide 68

- Follicular carcinoma is the next most common form of thyroid cancer, accounting for 10% to 20% of all thyroid cancers. It is more common in females and older patients,. Compared with papillary cancer, follicular carcinoma grows faster and is more likely to spread hematogenously, sometimes metastasizing to bones or lungs.
- Risk Factors and Pathogenesis. Follicular carcinoma is associated with RAS oncogene gain-of-function mutations.

## Slide 69

- Medullary Carcinoma
- Medullary carcinoma makes up only 1% to 2% of all thyroid cancer diagnoses. Its prognosis is worse than the previously discussed types, and it often has metastasized to the liver, lungs, or bones at the time of diagnosis.
- Risk Factors and Pathogenesis. About 25% of medullary cancers are genetically derived, usually associated with the RET oncogene mutations. This includes some patients with the genetic syndromes multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN) types 2A and 2B, who are at high risk of developing medullary carcinoma.
- MEN2A is characterized by medullary thyroid cancer, pheochromocytoma, and parathyroid adenomas.
- MEN2B is characterized by medullary thyroid cancer, pheochromocytoma, mucosal neuromas, and marfanoid features.
- Medullary carcinoma is derived from parafollicular cells (C cells), and it secretes calcitonin. Calcitonin is a sensitive tumor marker

## Slide 73

- Development
- The parathyroid glands arise from the endoderm of the third and fourth pharyngeal pouches. The third pharyngeal pouch gives rise to the inferior parathyroids, and the fourth pharyngeal pouch gives rise to the superior parathyroids.
- An abnormal migration may lead to ectopic placement of the gland (eg, inferior parathyroid glands in the anterior mediastinum with the thymus).
- DiGeorge syndrome (also referred to as 22q11) occurs by a similar process and leads to congenital thymic aplasia and absent parathyroid glands. Patients have hypocalcemia from hypoparathyroidism, and the absent thymus leads to frequent infections resulting from a lack of T cells.

## Slide 85

- The TRH receptors on the anterior pituitary and the TSH receptors on the thyroid are both G protein–coupled receptors.
- Receptors. Once they reach the target cell, thyroid hormones cannot passively diffuse through the cell membrane like steroid hormones can, even though T3 and T4 are lipophilic. Instead, they are internalized via specific transmembrane thyroid hormone transporters. Unlike steroid hormones, T4 and T3 do not bind to cytosolic receptors but instead go directly to the nucleus, where the thyroid hormone receptors are located. These receptors are found in cells throughout the body. Once inside the nucleus, T4 and T3 bind to specific receptors to modulate gene expression
- Thyroid hormones have major metabolic effects. At the cellular level, T3 and T4 upregulate the Na+/K+-ATPase, which increases cellular oxygen consumption, heat production, and basal metabolic rate.
- Thyroid hormones upregulate β1-adrenergic receptors in the heart, which increases the heart’s response to sympathetic activation. This leads to an elevation in pulse rate and cardiac output.
