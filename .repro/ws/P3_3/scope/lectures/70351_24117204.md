## Slide 8: Estrogen

- Binds intracellular estrogen receptors ERα and ERβ.
- The hormone–receptor complex binds DNA response elements and alters transcription.
- Stabilizes hypothalamic thermoregulation.
- Stimulates endometrial proliferation.
- Reduces osteoclast-mediated bone resorption.

## Slide 9: Progestogen

- Progestogen includes endogenous progesterone and synthetic progestins such as medroxyprogesterone acetate.
- Binds intracellular progesterone receptors.
- Opposes estrogen-induced endometrial proliferation.
- Promotes secretory endometrial differentiation.

## Slide 10: Characteristic Estrogen only Estrogen plus progestogen

| Characteristic | Estrogen only | Estrogen plus progestogen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Appropriate patient | Prior hysterectomy | Intact uterus |
| Primary indication | Moderate-to-severe VMS when systemic HT is appropriate | Same, with endometrial protection |
| Major benefit | Most effective VMS treatment without unnecessary progestogen exposure | VMS relief while preventing endometrial hyperplasia |
| Principal unique risk | Endometrial hyperplasia and cancer if used with an intact uterus | Additional bleeding, breast tenderness, bloating, and possible mood effects |

## Slide 11: Endometrial protection

- Continuous combined: estrogen and progestogen daily
- Cyclic: estrogen daily with progestogen for approximately 12–14 days each month
- Conjugated estrogen plus bazedoxifene: bazedoxifene acts as the endometrial antagonist
- Levonorgestrel intrauterine system may provide endometrial protection in selected patients, depending on product and clinical context
- Cyclic regimens usually produce scheduled withdrawal bleeding. Continuous regimens may cause initial unscheduled bleeding followed by amenorrhea.

## Slide 12: Estrogen routes

- Oral estradiol or conjugated equine estrogen
- Transdermal estradiol patch, gel, or spray
- Vaginal estrogen for GSM, not VMS

## Slide 13: Local (Vaginal) Estrogen & Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause (GSM)

- Clinical Indication:
- Specifically indicated for GSM: vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, urinary urgency, and recurrent UTIs
- Pharmacokinetics & Systemic Exposure:
- Low-dose vaginal formulations (creams, inserts/tablets, low-dose rings) maintain serum estradiol within the postmenopausal basal range (4-14pg/mL)
- Negligible systemic absorption; no observed increased risk of VTE, stroke, CHD, or breast cancer recurrence
- Endometrial Safety:
- Opposing progestogen therapy is NOT required for low-dose local vaginal estrogen.
- Clinical Utility:
- Can be continued long-term and used safely even when systemic MHT is contraindicated or discontinued

## Slide 14: Transdermal therapy

- It may be preferred when systemic HT remains appropriate in patients with:
- Hypertriglyceridemia
- Gallbladder disease
- Migraine
- Moderate cardiometabolic or VTE risk

## Slide 15: Oral and transdermal estrogen

| Oral estrogen | Transdermal estradiol |
| --- | --- |
| Undergoes hepatic first-pass metabolism | Largely bypasses hepatic first-pass exposure |
| Increases hepatic clotting-factor production | Less hepatic effect on coagulation proteins |
| Increases triglycerides | Less effect on triglycerides |
| Increases SHBG, TBG and CBG | Smaller effect on binding proteins |
| Greater gallbladder effect | Lower observed gallbladder risk |
| Higher observed VTE risk | Lower VTE risk in observational studies |

- Transdermal estrogen as risk-free. The route evidence comes mainly from observational studies.

## Slide 16: Modern MHT Dosing Strategy: "Start Low, Titrate Up”

- Shift in Clinical Practice: Historical Approach: Fixed "standard" starting doses (e.g., CEE 0.625mg/day or oral estradiol 1mg/day).
- Current Guideline Approach: Initiate therapy at lower doses (e.g., transdermal estradiol 0.025mg/day or oral estradiol 0.5 mg/day) and titrate upward based on symptom response

## Slide 17: Modern MHT Dosing Strategy: "Start Low, Titrate Up”- (Continued)

- Clinical Advantages of Low-Dose Initiation:
  - Effectively eliminates or reduces vasomotor symptoms in the majority of patients.
  - Lower adverse effect profile: Significantly reduces rates of breast tenderness, fluid retention, and unscheduled vaginal bleeding.
  - Improved safety markers: Exerts fewer effects on hepatic coagulation factors and inflammatory markers.
- Duration & Tapering:
- Re-evaluate periodically; when discontinuing, taper gradually (e.g., reducing by 1 pill/patch increment per week) to minimize rebound hot flashes

## Slide 18: Contraindications to systemic hormone therapy

- Systemic HT should generally be avoided with:
  - Current or previous breast cancer
  - Known coronary heart disease
  - Previous stroke or TIA
  - Previous VTE or a major thrombophilia
  - Active liver disease
  - Unexplained vaginal bleeding until evaluated
  - High-risk endometrial cancer
  - Pregnancy

## Slide 19: Contraindications to systemic hormone therapy-

- A different route does not automatically overcome a major contraindication.
- Low-dose vaginal estrogen has low, formulation-dependent systemic absorption. It should be distinguished from systemic therapy, but decisions in patients with estrogen-dependent cancer require individualized discussion.

  - Vaginal bleeding

## Slide 21: Adverse effects and monitoring-

- Progestogen-related effects

  - Breast tenderness
  - Bloating
  - Mood symptoms

## Slide 22: Risks

- VTE and pulmonary embolism
- Ischemic stroke
- Gallbladder disease
- Endometrial hyperplasia or cancer with unopposed systemic estrogen
- Regimen- and duration-dependent breast-cancer risk
- New or persistent bleeding after initial treatment adjustment requires evaluation rather than automatic attribution to hormone therapy

## Slide 28: Custom-Compounded Bioidentical Hormone Therapy (cBHT)

- Defining Terms:
- FDA-Approved Bioidenticals: Structurally identical to endogenous human hormones (17beta-estradiol, micronized progesterone) available in regulated commercial products
- Custom-Compounded (cBHT): Non-FDA-approved multi-hormone mixtures (creams, pellets, sublinguals) adjusted via salivary testing
- Safety & Quality Concerns:
- Lack of regulatory oversight for purity, potency, and batch consistency (tested potencies range from 67.5% to 268%of labeled strength)
- Unpredictable bioavailability and variable progestogen absorption, predisposing patients to unintended endometrial hyperplasia and carcinoma
- Professional Guidelines:
- The Menopause Society, Endocrine Society, and ACOG strongly advise against cBHT when FDA-approved options are available

## Slide 29: Individual risk stratification

- Before systemic HT:
  - Confirm that symptoms warrant systemic treatment.
  - Determine uterine status.
  - Evaluate unexplained bleeding.
  - Review breast-cancer and cardiovascular history.
  - Measure blood pressure.
  - Assess smoking, diabetes and lipid profile.
  - Review VTE history and thrombophilia.
  - Assess liver disease.
  - Review current medications and interactions.

## Slide 30: General principle:

- For appropriate candidates, benefit–risk is generally most favorable before age 60 or within 10 years of menopause.
- Age and timing do not override contraindications.
- 10-year CVD risk below 5%: generally low
- 5–10%: intermediate; consider transdermal therapy if HT remains appropriate
- Above 10%: generally favor nonhormonal treatment

## Slide 31: Special Populations: Primary Ovarian Insufficiency (POI) & Surgical Menopause

- Clinical Definitions:
  - Primary Ovarian Insufficiency (POI): Loss of ovarian function before age 40.
  - Early/Surgical Menopause: Menopause or bilateral oophorectomy before age 45
- Dosing & Therapeutic Goals:
- Require higher replacement doses (e.g., transdermal estradiol 0.1mg/day or oral estradiol 2mg/day) to restore physiological premenopausal levels.
- Terminology: Classically termed Hormone Replacement Therapy (HRT) rather than MHT
- Recommended Duration:
- Continue therapy until at least the average age of natural menopause ( 50-51years).
- Primary Indications:
- Essential for primary prevention of premature bone loss, coronary heart disease, ischemic stroke, and cognitive impairment.
- Crucial Distinction: WHI safety risks in older women (mean age 63) do NOT apply to POI

## Slide 32: Selective Estrogen Receptor Modulators (SERMs)

- Mechanism of Action:
- Bind intracellular estrogen receptors, displaying tissue-selective agonism or antagonism depending on target organ expression.
- High-Yield SERM Agents:
- Raloxifene: Estrogen agonist in bone (increases bone mineral density, reduces vertebral fractures) and antagonist in breast & endometrium. Approved for postmenopausal osteoporosis and breast cancer risk reduction. (No increased risk of endometrial cancer).
- Tamoxifen: Estrogen antagonist in breast, but partial agonist in endometrium and bone. Used in ER+ breast cancer. (Carries increased risk of endometrial hyperplasia/carcinoma and VTE).
- Bazedoxifene (+ CEE): Estrogen antagonist in the uterus; protects the endometrium without requiring a progestogen.
- Clomiphene: Hypothalamic estrogen receptor antagonist; blocks negative feedback to increase GnRH, LH, and FSH to induce ovulation.
- Shared Adverse Effects:
- Increased risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) and hot flashes (except bazedoxifene + CEE)

## Slide 33: Menopause, MHT, & Bone Health

- Bone Loss Trajectory:
  - Estrogen depletion accelerates RANKL-mediated osteoclast resorption, leading to a loss of 15–20% of bone density in the 5–7 years surrounding the menopausal transition.
- Fracture Reduction Efficacy:
  - Systemic MHT reduces vertebral, hip, and total osteoporotic fractures by 30–50% during active treatment.
- Guideline :
  - Secondary Benefit: Bone preservation is a valuable secondary benefit when treating VMS.
  - Not First-Line Solely for Osteoporosis: MHT is not recommended as first-line primary therapy solely for isolated chronic osteoporosis prevention in asymptomatic women (bisphosphonates preferred).
  - Rebound Loss: Discontinuing MHT leads to rapid resumption of bone loss, returning to untreated baseline trajectory within a few years.
- Selective Estrogen Receptor Modulators (SERMs):
  - Raloxifene: Estrogen agonist in bone (increases BMD, reduces vertebral fractures) and antagonist in breast/endometrium.
  - CEE + Bazedoxifene: Preserves bone density and protects the endometrium without requiring a progestogen

## Slide 34: Androgen Therapy in Menopausal Pharmacology

- Physiologic Context:
  - Endogenous testosterone levels decline gradually with age and sharply after bilateral oophorectomy.
- Primary Clinical Indication:
  - Hypoactive Sexual Desire Disorder (HSDD) in postmenopausal women when MHT alone provides incomplete relief.
- Regulatory Status & Prescribing:
  - No FDA-approved female testosterone product currently exists in the US (approved in UK/Australia).
- Clinical practice utilizes off-label micro-dosing of transdermal male preparations or compounded products under specialist care.
- Adverse Effects & Safety:
- Potential Effects: Acne, hirsutism, fluid retention, and adverse lipid changes (decreased HDL, increased LDL).

## Slide 37: Gabapentin

- Binds the auxiliary subunit of voltage-gated calcium channels.
- The precise mechanism for VMS relief remains uncertain.
- Useful for predominantly nocturnal VMS, insomnia, or concurrent neuropathic pain.
- Risks: sedation, dizziness and ataxia.
- Requires renal dose adjustment.
- It is not a GABA-receptor agonist.

## Slide 38: Oxybutynin

- Competitive muscarinic-receptor antagonist.
- Can reduce VMS and may benefit concurrent overactive bladder.
- Risks: dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention and cognitive adverse effects.
- Avoid or minimize in older adults with high anticholinergic burden.

## Slide 39: Clonidine

- Central α2-adrenergic agonist.
- Modest efficacy with hypotension, dry mouth, sedation and rebound hypertension.
- Generally not recommended routinely for VMS.

## Slide 41: Comorbidity-based treatment selection

| Clinical situation | Most appropriate direction |
| --- | --- |
| Healthy patient with prior hysterectomy | Estrogen alone |
| Intact uterus and appropriate HT candidate | Estrogen plus endometrial protection |
| Previous breast cancer | Nonhormonal treatment |
| Tamoxifen treatment | Venlafaxine, gabapentin, or appropriate neurokinin antagonist; avoid paroxetine/fluoxetine |
| Previous VTE, stroke, or CHD | Generally avoid systemic HT |
| Hypertriglyceridemia or gallbladder risk | Transdermal rather than oral estrogen if HT remains appropriate |
| Uncontrolled hypertension | Control BP before systemic HT; use SNRIs cautiously |
| Liver disease | Avoid systemic HT; assess restrictions for neurokinin antagonists |
| Severe renal impairment | Renally adjust gabapentin; avoid fezolinetant |
| High anticholinergic burden | Avoid oxybutynin |
| Nocturnal VMS with neuropathic pain | Gabapentin may be useful |
