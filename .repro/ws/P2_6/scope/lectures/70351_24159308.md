Capacitation is the step in the maturation of sperm that is required to fertilize an oocyte.
This step is a biochemical event; the sperm move normally and look mature prior to capacitation.
In vivo, capacitation occurs after ejaculation, when the sperms leave the vagina and enter the female
reproductive system. The uterus aids in the steps of capacitation by lipoproteins, proteolytic enzymes,
and glycosidase enzymes.

## Page 9

Explanation
Capacitation is a functional maturation process that occurs within the female
reproductive tract.
During this process, sperm undergo membrane and metabolic changes that enable
binding to the zona pellucida and initiation of the acrosome reaction.
Without capacitation, sperm may exhibit normal count, morphology, motility, and
transport through the reproductive tract, but they cannot penetrate the zona
pellucida and fertilize the oocyte.
Takeaway:
Capacitation → Acrosome reaction → Zona penetration → Cortical reaction
→ Completion of meiosis II → Pronuclear fusion.
Failure of capacitation is a classic cause of infertility despite otherwise normal
semen analysis.

## Page 14

Explanation
Following implantation, the trophoblast differentiates into two layers:
• Cytotrophoblast: the inner cellular layer that retains mitotic activity and gives rise to new
trophoblastic cells.
• Syncytiotrophoblast: the outer multinucleated layer that invades the endometrium, erodes
maternal capillaries, and establishes the early uteroplacental circulation.
• The syncytiotrophoblast also secretes human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), which
maintains the corpus luteum during early pregnancy. Failure of syncytiotrophoblast
invasion can lead to implantation failure, early pregnancy loss, and abnormal placentation.
Takeaway:
• Syncytiotrophoblast = implantation + invasion + hCG production.
• A useful association is:
Cytotrophoblast → proliferates
Syncytiotrophoblast → invades and secretes hCG

A. Placenta accreta
B. Placental abruption
C. Placenta previa
D. Velamentous placenta
E. Membranous placenta
A

## Page 20

Explanation
Placenta accreta occurs when there is defective formation of the decidua basalis,
allowing chorionic villi to attach directly to the myometrium. This abnormal
adherence prevents normal placental separation after delivery and can result in life-
threatening postpartum hemorrhage. Risk factors include: previous cesarean
delivery, placenta previa, prior uterine surgery, multiparity.
• Placenta previa: placenta overlies or is near the internal cervical os and presents
with painless third-trimester vaginal bleeding before delivery.
• Placental abruption: premature separation of a normally implanted placenta and
presents with painful vaginal bleeding, uterine tenderness, and fetal distress.
• Velamentous placenta: umbilical vessels travel within the membranes before
reaching the placenta and is associated with vasa previa and fetal hemorrhage.
• Membranous placenta (placenta membranacea): rare condition in which villi
persist over a large portion of the chorion and does not characteristically cause
failure of placental separation after delivery.

Explanation
• Preeclampsia is caused by abnormal placentation,
specifically inadequate invasion of the maternal
spiral arteries by trophoblasts.
• During normal development, trophoblastic cells

them from small, high-resistance vessels into large,

Primordial germ cells (PGCs) are the
precursors of spermatozoa and oocytes.
They originate in the wall of the yolk sac near
the allantois and subsequently migrate through
the dorsal mesentery to the developing gonadal
ridges during weeks 4 to 6 of development.

## Page 38

Explanation
• The SRY (Sex-determining Region of the Y chromosome) gene is the testis-
determining gene located on the short arm of the Y chromosome. Its protein
product functions as a transcription factor that initiates differentiation of the
indifferent gonad into a testis.
• The earliest major effect of SRY expression is the development of Sertoli cells
from the primitive sex cords. Sertoli cells subsequently secrete anti-Müllerian
hormone (AMH), causing regression of the Müllerian ducts.
A developmental sequence to remember is:
• SRY expression → Testis formation → Leydig cell differentiation →
Testosterone production → Wolffian duct development.
• SRY expression → Sertoli cell differentiation → AMH secretion → Müllerian
duct regression

Takeaway
AIS = 46,XY + testes + AMH present + androgen receptor defect
• AMH → no uterus, no fallopian tubes, blind-ending vagina
• Androgen insensitivity → female external genitalia
• Testosterone aromatization → normal breast development
• Lack of androgen action → sparse pubic and axillary hair
• This combination of primary amenorrhea + normal breasts + sparse pubic hair +
blind-ending vagina + absent uterus + 46,XY is the classic presentation of
Complete Androgen Insensitivity Syndrome (CAIS)

## Page 61

Androgen Insensitivity Syndrome
• AIS is caused by a mutation in the androgen receptor,
resulting in complete resistance to testosterone and
dihydrotestosterone (DHT). Although the testes
develop normally under the influence of the SRY
gene, the body's tissues cannot respond to androgens.
• Sertoli cells produce anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH),
causing regression of the Müllerian ducts and
resulting in the absence of the uterus and fallopian
tubes.
• Testosterone is aromatized to estrogen, leading to
normal breast development.
