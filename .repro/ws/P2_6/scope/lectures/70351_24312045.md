## Page 9

Molar pregnancy: Risk Factors
• Extremes of age (<15 yo or >45 yo)
• Risk is 5–10 times greater for > 45 yo
• History of prior molar pregnancy
• Second molar pregnancy: risk 1–2% (vs 0.1%)
• Third molar pregnancy: risk 15–20%
• Risk is much higher after a complete mole
• Nulliparity
• Familial recurrent hydatidiform mole syndrome (FRHM)
• Autosomal recessive NLRP7 mutation
• Incidence: 1-2/1000 pregnancies in western countries
• (2-3 times higher in Asia, Brazil, India)

## Page 19

Molar Pregnancy: Treatment 101
• Medical management (e.g., misoprostol, mifepristone) ineffective, risks
hemorrhage, and progression to GTN requiring chemotherapy
• Simple hysterectomy is alternative if fertility is not desired (especially if age >40yo)
• RhoGam if needed
• Contraception – what kind? Why is this critical?
• Follow hCG levels to zero and trend for ~6 months given risk of progression to
invasive disease / choriocarcinoma
• Managed by an OB/GYN

## Page 24

Beta hCG and TSH
• Elevated circulating levels of hCG seen in complete molar
pregnancy can increase T4 secretion, which, in turn,
suppresses TSH.
• hCG and TSH receptors exhibit similar homology of
approximately 70% for the transmembrane domain and
45% for the extracellular domains of the receptors.
• Hyperthyroidism is known to be associated with GTDs
because of this cross-reactivity and significantly higher and
sustained β-hCG levels compared to normal pregnancy.

## Page 25

Gestational Trophoblastic Disease: Epidemiology
• 80% of molar pregnancies do not progress to invasive disease
• Most go on to have normal subsequent pregnancies without cancer
• 20% progress to invasive disease:
• Locally invasive
• Metastatic
• Molar Pregnancy
• Baseline incidence = 0.001%
• Risk of recurrence after 1 mole= 1%
• Risk of recurrence after 2 subsequent moles = 23%

## Page 26

Malignant Gestational Trophoblastic Disease
• Managed by a gynecologic oncologist
• Three types of malignant histology:
• Persistent / invasive moles (75%)
• Choriocarcinoma (25%)
• Placental Site Trophoblastic Tumor (<1%, extremely rare)
• Epithelioid trophoblastic tumor (ETT) (<1%, even rarer)

## Page 29

Methotrexate
• How does methotrexate work?
• Folate antimetabolite that inhibits DNA synthesis,
repair, and cellular replication.
• Binds to and inhibits dihydrofolate reductase
• Inhibits formation of purine and thymidylic acid
synthesis
• Interfers with DNA synthesis, repair, and cellular
replication.
• Methotrexate is cell cycle specific for the S phase of
the cycle.
• Actively proliferative tissues are more susceptible to
the effects of methotrexate.

## Page 30

Choriocarcinoma
• Hematogenous spread, vascular invasion
• Lungs, Vagina, Pelvis
• Brain, Liver, Intestines
• A rare metastatic cancer that can be cured
• Elevated beta hCG (tumor marker)
• Arises after a normal pregnancy, abortion, or ectopic
pregnancy
• Presentation: late post-partum bleeding
• If arises from gestational pathway (this one!)
• Chemo-sensitive
• Ovarian germ cell pathway
• Chemo-resistant
• Treated with multi-agent chemotherapy; fertility spared
Ellenson LH & Pirog EC. The Female Genital Tract. In Kumar V, Abbas AK, Fausto N, et al. In Robbins
and Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease. Eighth Edition. Philadelphia, PA: Saunders ElSevier; 2010.

Side-note: Non-Gestational Choriocarcinoma
• Extremely rare
• Germ cell tumor (ovaries and testes)
• Can’t distinguish gestational vs non-gestational on IHC or tumor markers-
need tumoral genetics
• Ovarian: poor prognosis. Do not respond to chemo.
• You can also technically have a gestational choriocarcinoma of the ovary
(metastasis or ovarian pregnancy)

Placental-Site Trophoblastic Tumor
(PSTT)
• Rare (<2%) of GTN
• Neoplasm of intermediate trophoblasts
(normally found at the implantation site)
• Presentation:
• Uterine mass
• Abnormal uterine bleeding or amenorrhea
• Moderately elevated hCG
• 85% have local disease (excellent prognosis)

## Page 33

Placental Site Trophoblastic Tumor (PSTT)
• Not usually post-molar
• Often diagnosed 18 mos after term pregnancy
• Slow growing
• Those with local disease are cured with
hysterectomy +/- lymphadenectomy
• High risk disease may require adjuvant multi-
agent chemo
• Metastases are rare, but treated with debulking
and multiagent chemo
• Less sensitive to chemo
• About 15% die of disease

## Page 34

Epithelioid trophoblastic tumor (ETT)
• Extremely rare
• Incidence: 0.1 per 100,000 deliveries
• +hCG (<3000 mIU/ml)
• Arise from chorionic-type intermediate trophoblast cells
• Not usually post-molar (avg 6 yrs post preg)
Pathologic Features:
• Nodular growth, deep invasion
• Mild nuclear atypia
• “geographic necrosis”
• Treated with surgery (hysterectomy) +/- multiagent chemotherapy
• Metastasis in 25%
• Less sensitive to chemo
• Half die of disease
