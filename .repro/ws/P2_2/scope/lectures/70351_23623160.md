Ovary: Structure and Function
LO: Describe the microscopic organization of the ovary, uterine tube, and uterus
Relate histological changes to the ovarian cycle, menstrual cycle, and pregnancy
Microscopic organization:
Cortex: Contains follicles at various stages of development,
the corpus luteum, and corpora albicantia, embedded in a
cellular stroma.
Medulla: Loose connective tissue carrying blood vessels,
lymphatics, and nerves.
Tunica albuginea: Dense connective tissue layer beneath the
surface epithelium.
Surface epithelium: Simple cuboidal to squamous
mesothelium (historically misnamed "germinal epithelium").
Oogenesis:
Begins in fetal life (~11–12 weeks' gestation); oogonia enter
meiosis I and arrest as primary oocytes in prophase I until
puberty.
The LH surge resumes meiosis I, yielding a secondary oocyte
plus first polar body; the secondary oocyte is released at
ovulation and arrests in metaphase II.
Meiosis II completes only if fertilization occurs, extruding the
second polar body.

• Primordial follicles: Primary oocyte surrounded by a

Primary follicles: Cuboidal granulosa cells; zona pellucida

Endometriosis
LO: Explain how altered structure/function leads to clinical consequences (e.g., follicular cysts, tubal infertility, endometriosis,
abnormal uterine bleeding, menopause).
Endometriosis is defined by the presence of endometrial glands and stroma in a location outside the uterus
Multifocal and involves pelvic structures (e.g., ovaries, pouch of Douglas, uterine ligaments, fallopian tubes)
• Less frequently, involves distant areas of the peritoneal cavity, periumbilical tissues, or laparotomy scars
• There are three types of endometriosis:
• superficial peritoneal endometriosis
• ovarian endometriosis
• deep infiltrating endometriosis
• Risk of malignant transformation is mainly confined to deep infiltrating endometriosis
lesions that penetrate more than 5 mm beneath the peritoneal surface, often involving structures like the
bowel, bladder, and uterosacral ligaments.
More prone to genetic mutations and cellular changes that may lead to malignancy

Endometriosis-Pathogenesis
Pathogenesis
1. origin from the uterine endometrium
2. origin from cells outside the uterus giving rise to endometrial tissue
The leading theories are as follows:
•The regurgitation theory: endometrial tissue implants at ectopic sites via retrograde
flow of menstrual endometrium through the opening of the fallopian tube
•The benign metastasis theory: endometrial tissue can “spread” to distant sites (e.g.,
bone, lung, and brain) via blood vessels and lymphatic channels
•The metaplastic theory: endometrium arises directly from coelomic epithelium
(mesothelium of pelvis or abdomen), from which the müllerian ducts and ultimately
the endometrium originate during embryonic development. Mesonephric remnants
may undergo endometrial differentiation and give rise to ectopic endometrial tissue.
•The extrauterine stem/progenitor cell theory: stem/progenitor cells from the bone
marrow differentiate into endometrial tissue.
Implants have different characteristics than eutopic uterine endometrium
Endometriotic tissue exhibits increased levels of proinflammatory and angiogenic
factors, including prostaglandin E 2, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and
matrix metalloproteinases (MMP), some of which are released by macrophages
that are recruited to endometriotic implants by proinflammatory factors
Endometriotic stromal cells make high levels of aromatase, leading to increased
local production of estrogen from androgens

Adenomyosis
LO: Explain the pathophysiology and clinicopathologic features of functional disorders, including polycystic ovary syndrome, endometrial
hyperplasia, adenomyosis, and endometriosis.
Definition
Adenomyosis is the presence of endometrial glands and stroma within the
myometrium (uterine muscle wall)
Pathophysiology
Endometrial tissue becomes embedded deep within the myometrium
between muscle bundles
This ectopic tissue induces reactive hypertrophy of surrounding smooth
muscle
Results in a globular, enlarged uterus with a thickened uterine wall
Clinical Features
Menorrhagia (heavy menstrual bleeding)
Dysmenorrhea (painful menstruation)
Pelvic pain, especially pre-menstrual
May coexist with endometriosis, but they are distinct entities.
Gross and Imaging Findings
Uterus appears enlarged and boggy
Wall may be diffusely thickened
Diagnosed via MRI or ultrasound, though definitive diagnosis may
require histology

Endometrial Hyperplasia
Definition & Pathogenesis
Endometrial hyperplasia is an abnormal proliferation of endometrial glands due to excess estrogen unopposed by
progesterone
It is a precursor lesion for Type I endometrial carcinoma (endometrioid type).
Etiologies of Estrogen Excess
Obesity (peripheral conversion of androgens to estrogens in adipose tissue)
Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)
Estrogen-producing ovarian tumors (e.g., granulosa cell tumors)
Exogenous estrogen therapy without progestin
Classification
1. Hyperplasia without atypia
Increased gland-to-stroma ratio
Glands may be cystically dilated
Low risk of progression to carcinoma (1–3%)
2. Hyperplasia with atypia (also called Endometrial Intraepithelial Neoplasia, EIN)
Complex glandular architecture
Nuclear atypia: enlarged, vesicular nuclei, prominent nucleoli
High risk of progression to carcinoma
Associated with PTEN mutations

Grade 3; B: solid growth, marked atypia
Clinical Features:
Presents with postmenopausal bleeding
Slow progression, often localized
Good prognosis if detected early (90% 5-year survival in early stages)

Uterine Leiomyoma (Fibroid)
LO: Compare benign and malignant uterine tumors (e.g., leiomyomas vs. leiomyosarcomas), including morphology, clinical course, and prognosis.
• Most common tumors in women
• Benign smooth muscle neoplasms; single or multiple
• Estrogen and progesterone stimulate leiomyoma growth; tumors shrink after menopause
• Associated with recurrent chromosomal abnormalities, including rearrangements of
chromosomes 6 and 12 found in other benign neoplasms (e.g., endometrial polyps and
lipomas)
• Mutations in the MED12 gene; encodes a protein that regulates RNA polymerase II-mediated
transcription, have been identified in up to 70% of leiomyomas
• The mechanism by which MED12 mutations contribute to the development of leiomyomas is
not understood.

Leiomyosarcoma
Uncommon malignant neoplasms
Arise from the myometrium or endometrial stromal precursor cells
Almost always solitary and most often occur in postmenopausal women, in contrast to leiomyomas;
leiomyomas are frequently multiple and usually arise before menopause
They have complex, highly variable karyotypes including chromosomal deletions
Like leiomyomas, they contain MED12 mutations but in a smaller subset (30%).
Morphology
Soft, hemorrhagic, necrotic masses
The histopathology is highly variable; from tumors resembling leiomyoma to wildly anaplastic neoplasms
The diagnostic features of leiomyosarcoma include tumor necrosis, cytologic atypia, and mitotic activity
Because increased mitotic activity is sometimes seen in benign smooth muscle tumors, particularly in young
women, an assessment of all three features is necessary to make a diagnosis of malignancy

Serous tubal intraepithelial carcinoma
1. Site of Origin for High-Grade Serous Carcinoma
STIC lesions in the fallopian tube, particularly at the fimbriated end, are now recognized as the precursor lesions for many high-grade serous
carcinomas previously thought to originate in the ovary.
This has shifted the paradigm from “ovarian origin” to fallopian tube origin for these aggressive tumors.
2. Molecular Features
>90% of STICs harbor TP53 mutations, similar to uterine serous carcinoma.
These mutations are a hallmark of high-grade serous neoplasia.
3. Association with BRCA Mutations
STICs are frequently found in prophylactically removed fallopian tubes from women with BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations.
Less commonly seen in women without known genetic risk factors.
4. Clinical Implications
Prophylactic salpingo-oophorectomy in BRCA mutation carriers can detect STICs before invasive carcinoma develops.
Early identification may prevent progression to high-grade serous carcinoma involving the ovary, omentum, and peritoneal cavity.
5. Histological Features
Atypical epithelial cells with:
High nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio
Strong p53 immunostaining
No evidence of stromal invasion in STIC.

Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS)-Pathophysiology
LO: Explain the pathophysiology and clinicopathologic features of functional disorders, including polycystic ovary syndrome, endometrial
hyperplasia, adenomyosis, and endometriosis.
Pathophysiology: (↑LH, ↓ FSH, ↑ androstenedione, ↓estradiol, ↑estrone)
1. Underlying Neuroendocrine Dysregulation
The hypothalamic-pituitary axis in PCOS is intrinsically dysregulated, possibly due to genetic and environmental factors.
This leads to increased GnRH pulse frequency, which favors LH secretion over FSH; LH:FSH ratio (often >2:1)
2. Androgen Feedback Loop
LH stimulates theca cells → ovarian thecal overactivity--↑ androgen production (e.g., testosterone, androstenedione)--- ↓ FSH
(negative feedback loop)
↓ FSH → Impaired Granulosa Cell Function (↓ aromatase activity in granulosa cells); Androgens are not efficiently converted to
estrogens → follicular arrest (first meiosis) and anovulation
This selective feedback maintains or further increases GnRH pulse frequency, reinforcing LH dominance.
3. Peripheral Conversion of Androgens to Estrogens
Androgens →estrone via aromatization in adipose tissue
Leads to chronic low-level estrogen without progesterone →endometrial hyperplasia or carcinoma
4. Insulin Resistance and Hyperinsulinemia
Insulin acts synergistically with LH to stimulate theca cells →more androgen production.
Insulin also reduces SHBG (Sex Hormone Binding Globulin) → ↑free androgens, which perpetuate the feedback loop.
5. Absence of Progesterone Feedback
Due to anovulation, there is no corpus luteum →no progesterone---- menstrual irregularity and unopposed estrogen effects.
Progesterone normally slows GnRH pulses; its absence allows faster GnRH pulsate
