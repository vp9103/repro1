## Slide 11: Spermatogenesis-Definition and Phases

- LO: Explain the processes and regulation of spermatogenesis and spermiogenesis.
- Spermatogenesis is the process of producing haploid sperm cells from diploid germ cells in the seminiferous tubules
- Three main phases: Spermatocytogenesis, Spermatidogenesis, and Spermiogenesis
- A final step, spermiation, involves the release of mature sperm
- Spermatocytogenesis
- Begins with spermatogonia (stem cells) located in the basal compartment of seminiferous tubules
- Types of spermatogonia:
  - Type A dark (AD): stem cell maintenance
  - Type A pale (AP): differentiates into Type B
  - Type B: undergoes mitosis to form primary spermatocytes
- Primary spermatocytes undergo meiosis I to form secondary spermatocytes.
- Spermatidogenesis
- Secondary spermatocytes undergo meiosis II to produce haploid spermatids
- Spermiogenesis
- Aniebo Umoh, E. (2024). Spermatogenesis. IntechOpen. doi: 10.5772/intechopen.1007771

## Slide 12: Spermatogenesis: Spermiogenesis, maturation, and spermiation

- LO: Explain the processes and regulation of spermatogenesis and spermiogenesis.
- Spermiogenesis
- Transformation of spermatids into mature spermatozoa.
- Golgi phase: acrosome formation begins
- Cap phase: acrosomal cap forms
- Tail formation: development of flagellum
- Maturation phase:
- cytoplasm removal by Sertoli cells
- Key organelles involved: Golgi apparatus, mitochondria, nucleus, centrioles
- Spermiation
- Final release of mature spermatozoa into the lumen of seminiferous tubules.
- Facilitated by Sertoli cells.
- Aniebo Umoh, E. (2024). Spermatogenesis. IntechOpen. doi: 10.5772/intechopen.1007771
- Structure of Sperm - Sperm cells are divided into a head, containing DNA; a midpiece, containing mitochondria; and a tail, providing motility. The acrosome is oval and flattened

## Slide 13: Spermatogenesis: Regulation and Clinical Relevance

- LO: Explain the processes and regulation of spermatogenesis and spermiogenesis.
- Regulation of Spermatogenesis
- FSH stimulates Sertoli cells
- LH stimulates Leydig cells to produce testosterone
- Testosterone is essential for all stages.
- Calcium signaling also plays a role in regulation.
- Influenced by temperature, toxins, medications, and genetic factors.
- Clinical Relevance
- Disruption in spermatogenesis can lead to:
- Infertility
- Genetic disorders (e.g., Klinefelter syndrome, Down syndrome)
- Spontaneous abortion

## Slide 15: Histology of Epididymis

- LO: Correlate the microscopic organization and function of the rete testis, ductuli efferentes, epididymis, and ductus deferens, seminal vesicles, prostate gland, and bulbourethral glands.
- Location: Posterior side of each testis.
- Connection: Receives sperm from the rete testis via efferent ductules.
- Function: Site of sperm maturation, fluid absorption, and storage.
- Regions of the Epididymis:
  - Head (Caput); Connected to the upper testis; Receives immature, non-motile sperm
  - Body (Corpus): Highly convoluted tubule; Site of sperm maturation and motility development
  - Tail (Cauda): Connects to the vas deferens; Stores fully mature, motile sperm
- Histology of the Epididymis: Pseudostratified columnar:
  - Principal Cells: Have stereocilia (long, non-motile microvilli); Absorb excess fluid; Secrete glycerophosphocholine (supports sperm motility)
  - Basal cells: Stem cells for epithelial renewal
  - Clear (halo) cells: May assist in fluid reabsorption
- Numerous sperm in the lumen. The Principal cells demonstrate apical, giant microvilli known as stereocilia. Basal cells (Upward Pointing Arrow) are present at the base of the epithelium. A thin layer of smooth muscle cells (Left Pointing Arrow) aids in the forward movement of sperm

## Slide 23: BPH: Overview-Pathogenesis

- LO: Explain the molecular and hormonal origins of prostatic nodular hyperplasia, the area of the gland affected, the natural history of the disease, various treatment strategies, and anticipated outcomes of treatment.
- Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH):
- Most common benign prostatic disease in men >50 years.
- Prevalence increases with age; affects up to 90% of men by age 80.
- Pathogenesis
- Androgen-dependent proliferation of stromal and glandular elements.
- Dihydrotestosterone (DHT) is the key mediator:
  - Synthesized from testosterone via 5α-reductase type 2.
  - Binds androgen receptors to promote growth and survival of prostate cells.
- Estrogens may enhance DHT effects, especially as testosterone declines with age.
- Hormonal Influence: Does not occur in:
  - Males castrated before puberty.
  - Males with genetic defects in androgen signaling.
- DHT increases stromal cell proliferation and reduces epithelial cell apoptosis.
- Kumar, Vinay, MBBS, MD, FRCPath, Robbins & Kumar Basic Pathology, 16, 582-601

## Slide 25: Prostatitis

- LO: Describe the pathophysiologic basis for inflammatory conditions affecting the prostate, including the causative organisms.
- Types of Prostatitis
- 1. Acute Bacterial Prostatitis
- Sudden onset: fever, chills, dysuria, perineal pain, urinary obstruction
- Caused by common uropathogens (e.g., E. coli)
- Digital rectal exam is contraindicated due to risk of bacteremia
- Diagnosis: urine culture, clinical features
- Treated with antibiotics
- 2. Chronic Bacterial Prostatitis
- Recurrent UTIs with asymptomatic intervals
- Symptoms: low back pain, dysuria, suprapubic/perineal discomfort
- Diagnosis: leukocytes in expressed prostatic secretions, positive cultures
- Treated with antibiotics
- 3. Chronic Abacterial Prostatitis (Pelvic Pain Syndrome); Most common form
- Similar symptoms to chronic bacterial prostatitis
- No history of recurrent UTIs
- Cultures are negative, but secretions show >10 leukocytes/HPF
- Etiology unknown; diagnosis of exclusion
- Treatment is empirical, based on symptoms
- 4. Granulomatous Prostatitis
- May mimic prostate cancer due to induration
- Most common cause in the U.S.: BCG therapy for bladder cancer
- Other causes: fungal infections (in immunocompromised), nonspecific foreign-body reactions

## Slide 27: PCA: Epidemiology and Pathogenesis

- Explain the epidemiology of prostate cancer with respect to age and family history.
- Outline the cellular phenotype of the typical prostate adenocarcinoma cell and describe its molecular and immunohistochemical characteristics.
- Epidemiology & Clinical Significance
- Most common cancer in men and second leading cause of cancer death (after lung cancer).
- Incidence increases with age:
  - ~20% in men in their 50s
  - ~70% in men aged 70–80
- Most cases are asymptomatic, nonpalpable, and detected via needle biopsy following elevated PSA.
- Pathogenesis
- Androgens (especially DHT) are central to tumor growth and progression.
- Tumors may initially respond to androgen deprivation, but often develop resistance via:
  - Androgen receptor (AR) gene amplification or mutation
  - Activation of alternative signaling pathways
- Hereditary factors:
  - First-degree relatives → 2× increased risk
  - Germline mutations (e.g., BRCA2, MYC, mismatch repair genes) linked to aggressive disease
- Environmental factors:
  - Western diet (charred meats, animal fats), estrogens, oxidants may contribute
- Genetic alterations:
  - TMPRSS2–ETS fusion: common driver mutation
  - MYC amplification, PTEN deletion, TP53 loss, RB deletion
  - Epigenetic silencing of GSTP1, RB, MLH1, MSH2, APC

## Slide 28: PCA: Morphology

- LO: Define the histopathological diagnostic criteria for the diagnosis of PCA
- Morphology & Histology:
- 70% arise in the peripheral zone, often posteriorly → may be palpable on DRE
- Gross appearance: firm, solid, gritty, pale, lacking normal spongy texture (circle)
- Histologic features:
  - Small, crowded glands with single-layer epithelium
  - No basal cell layer (vs. BPH)
  - Enlarged nuclei with prominent nucleoli
  - HGPIN (high-grade prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia) seen in ~80% of cases

## Slide 29: PCA: Gleason Grading System

- LO: Define the histopathological diagnostic criteria for the diagnosis of PCA
- Morphology & Histology:
- Gleason grading system: Based on glandular architecture, not cytologic atypia.
- Grades 1–5:
  - Grade 1: Well-differentiated, uniform glands
  - Grade 5: Undifferentiated sheets, cords, or nests of cells
- Gleason Score = Primary grade + Secondary grade
  - Most treatable cancers: Score 6–7
  - Advanced/aggressive cancers: Score 8–10
- Gleason scores are now grouped into Grade Groups 1–5, each with distinct prognostic implications.
- Used alongside pathologic staging to guide treatment decisions.
- Low-grade prostate adenocarcinoma (Gleason score 1 + 1 = 2) consisting of back-to-back uniform-sized malignant glands lined by a single layer of epithelial cells.
- A focus of Gleason grade 4 prostatic adenocarcinoma displays large cribriform glands with multiple cellular bridges crossing the central lumina.
- Grade group 5 adenocarcinoma (Gleason score 5 + 5 = 10) composed of sheets of malignant cells with no identifiable gland formation.

## Slide 30: PCA: Diagnosis, Management, and Prognosis

- LO: Compare and contrast the significance of “histological” prostatic adenocarcinoma versus a “clinically significant” adenocarcinoma.
- Diagnosis
- PSA testing: Widely used but has limited sensitivity and specificity
- Elevated in BPH, prostatitis, and after instrumentation
- MRI can help detect nonpalpable cancers
- Serial PSA levels helpful in monitoring recurrence or progression
- Bone metastases:
- Common in late-stage disease
- Typically osteoblastic (bone-forming), seen on radionuclide bone scans
- Management & Prognosis
- Surgery, radiation, ± hormonal therapy
- 90% of treated patients have 15-year survival
- Androgen deprivation therapy (ADT): Effective short-term, but androgen-independent clones often emerge
  - Achieved via LHRH agonists
  - Initially stimulates LH → then suppresses it → ↓ testosterone
- Prognosis depends on:
- Pathologic stage
- Surgical margin status
- Gleason grade

## Slide 36: Peyronie disease:

- LO: Describe the underlying abnormality causing Peyronie disease, the clinical consequences, and its treatment.
- An acquired condition in middle-aged or older men.
- Characterized by fibrous plaque formation in the tunica albuginea (the fibrous sheath surrounding the corpora cavernosa). Leads to asymmetric erection, abnormal curvature, painful erections, and difficulty with intercourse.
- Clinical Consequences
- Penile curvature, especially during erection
- Painful erections
- Erectile dysfunction
- Difficulty with intercourse
- Psychological distress, including anxiety and depression
- Diagnosis
- Based on clinical history and physical exam
- Palpable plaque on the shaft of the penis
- Imaging (e.g., penile ultrasound) may be used to assess plaque size and calcification
- Treatment Options
- Treatment depends on severity, duration, and impact on function:
- Non-surgical options:
- Oral medications: Pentoxifylline (anti-fibrotic)
- Intralesional injections: Collagenase Clostridium histolyticum (FDA-approved)
- Verapamil or interferon (off-label)
- Shockwave therapy: (limited evidence)
- Surgical options (for severe or refractory cases)

## Slide 37: LO: Discuss the morphology, consequences, and management of phimosis, hypospadias, and epispadias.

- Hypospadias
- Urethral opening on the ventral surface of the penis; may be glanular, penile, or perineal.
- Incomplete fusion of urethral folds
- Associated anomalies: Inguinal hernia, cryptorchidism
- Abnormal urine stream, difficulty with urination and sexual function; Fertility issues if severe
- Complications: Constricted urethral orifice may lead to Urinary tract obstruction and Increased risk of urinary tract infections (UTIs)
- Surgical correction, typically performed between 6–18 months of age
- Avoid circumcision before repair (foreskin may be used in reconstruction)
- Epispadias
- Urethral opening on the dorsal surface of the penis
- Defective positioning of the genital tubercle
- Often associated with: Bladder exstrophy
- May cause urinary incontinence
- Complex surgical repair
- Phimosis
- Inability to retract the foreskin over the glans
- Congenital or acquired (e.g., inflammation, scarring): Balanitis: inflammation of the glans; Balanoposthitis: inflammation of the glans and prepuce, due to poor hygiene in uncircumcised males or accumulation of smegma (desquamated cells, sweat, debris)
- Results in hygiene issues: Increased risk of infection
- Painful erections
- Topical steroids
- Circumcision if severe or recurrent

## Slide 38: Testicular Torsion:

- LO: Describe the clinical features and pathologic findings in the testis that occur due to torsion of the spermatic cord
- Twisting of the spermatic cord
- Typically results in obstruction of testicular venous drainage while the thick-walled and more resilient arteries remain patent
- If untreated---engorgement and infarction
- Two types of testicular torsion:
  - Neonatal torsion ( in utero or shortly after birth); no associated anatomic defect
  - Adult torsion results from a bilateral anatomic defect in the anchoring of the testis in the scrotal sac that leads to its increased mobility; it typically occurs in adolescence and manifests with the sudden onset of testicular pain.
  - A urologic emergency
  - Manual untwisting within 6 hours of onset---testis remains viable.
  - Contralateral orchiopexy is performed to prevent recurrence in the unaffected testis.

## Slide 44: Cryptorchidism

- LO:
- Describe the complications observed for failure of the testes to descend (cryptorchidism).
- Describe the most important risk factors, genetic associations, and molecular basis for the development of testicular tumors and outline the clinicopathologic features for the different morphologic patterns seen.
- Cryptorchidism is a complete or partial failure of the intraabdominal testes to descend into the scrotal sac
- It is associated with testicular dysfunction and an increased risk of testicular cancer
- Cryptorchidism is found in approximately 1% of 1-year-old boys
- Normally, the testes descend from the abdominal cavity into the pelvis by the third month of gestation and then through the inguinal canals into the scrotum during the last 2 months of intrauterine life.
- The diagnosis of cryptorchidism is only established with certainty after 1 year of age particularly in premature infants
- Undescended testes become atrophic, bilateral cryptorchidism results in sterility.
- Even unilateral cryptorchidism may be associated with atrophy of the contralateral descended gonad.
- Patients with unilateral cryptorchidism are also at increased risk for the development of cancer in the contralateral, normally descended testis ( intrinsic abnormality, rather than simple failure of descent)
- Cryptorchid testes are small and firm.
- The histologic changes include thickening of the basement membrane, loss of spermatogonia, increased interstitial stroma, and relative prominence of Leydig cells.
- Germ cell neoplasia in situ may be present in cryptorchid testes; precursor of subsequent germ cell tumors.
- Current recommendations are for surgical repositioning (orchiopexy), to be performed at 6 to 12 months of age.

## Slide 45: Leydig Cell Tumor

- LO: Describe the most important risk factors, genetic associations, and molecular basis for the development of testicular tumors and outline the clinicopathologic features for the different morphologic patterns seen.
- Leydig Cell Tumor
- Most common sex cord-stromal tumor in children.
- Can occur at any age (children and adults)
- Presents with precocious puberty in boys (pubic hair, acne, rapid growth); Elevated testosterone.
- In adults: may cause gynecomastia; excess estrogen from androgen aromatization
- Painless, firm testicular mass; often unilateral.
- Histologic Features
  - Polygonal cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm.
  - Reinke crystals (rod-shaped intracytoplasmic inclusions)
  - No lymphocytic infiltrate or germ cell elements.
- Pathogenesis
  - Arises from Leydig (interstitial) cells of the testis.
  - Not associated with GCNIS or i(12p) abnormalities.
- Prognosis: Mostly benign
- Surgical excision is typically curative.

## Slide 46: Seminoma

- LO: Describe the most important risk factors, genetic associations, and molecular basis for the development of testicular tumors and outline the clinicopathologic features for the different morphologic patterns seen.
- A germ cell tumor presents as a soft, well-demarcated, gray-white, pale, fleshy tumor that bulges from the cut surface of the affected testis
- Large tumors may contain foci of coagulative necrosis, usually without hemorrhage
- It is composed of large, uniform cells with distinct cell borders, clear glycogen-rich cytoplasm, round nuclei, and conspicuous nucleoli
- The cells often are arrayed in small lobules with intervening fibrous septa.
- A lymphocytic infiltrate is usually present.
- In approximately 15% of cases, syncytiotrophoblasts are present; these cells are the source of the minimally elevated serum human choriogonadotropin (hCG) that is seen in 10% to 15% of patients with stage I disease, increasing to 30% to 50% in disseminated disease.
- Their presence has no bearing on prognosis, and the levels of hCG are usually much lower than in choriocarcinoma

## Slide 51: Penile Intraepithelial Neoplasia (PeIN)

- LO: Explain the spectrum of squamous neoplasia affecting the penis, ranging from condyloma acuminata to invasive squamous cell carcinoma, describing the risk factors, pathogenesis, morphologic features, complications, and treatment strategies.
- 2. Penile Intraepithelial Neoplasia (PeIN)
- Etiology: Associated with high-risk HPV types (especially HPV-16 and HPV-18).
- Morphology: Solitary plaque, often on the shaft or glans.
- Histology: Full-thickness epithelial atypia, nuclear pleomorphism, mitoses (arrows), intact basement membrane.
- Pathogenesis: Dysplastic transformation of squamous epithelium without stromal invasion.
- Complications: ~10% progress to invasive squamous cell carcinoma.
- Treatment: Topical chemotherapy (5-FU), laser ablation, surgical excision.

## Slide 52: Penile Invasive Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC)

- 3. Invasive Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC)
- Etiology: Strongly linked to HPV-16/18, poor hygiene, smoking, chronic inflammation, and lack of circumcision.
- Morphology: Ulcerated, indurated, crusted lesion on the glans or prepuce.
- Histology: Keratinizing squamous cells invading the stroma; nuclear atypia, mitotic figures.
- Pathogenesis: Progression from PeIN with basement membrane breach and stromal infiltration.
- Complications:
- Local tissue destruction.
- Lymphatic spread to inguinal and pelvic nodes.
- Poor prognosis with nodal metastasis.
- Treatment:
- Early-stage: Partial penectomy, Mohs surgery.
- Advanced: Total penectomy, lymphadenectomy, radiation, chemotherapy.
- Special Variant: Verrucous Carcinoma
- Etiology: Not HPV-related.
- Morphology: Papillary, exophytic growth with pushing margins.
- Histology: Minimal atypia, locally invasive but non-metastasizing.
- Treatment: Surgical excision; radiation avoided due to risk of anaplastic transformation.
