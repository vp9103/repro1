•
Provides a barrier and mucus secretion to regulate
sperm entry and protect the upper genital tract.

The ectocervix, like the vagina (i.e., the part of the

squamous epithelium

columnar mucous-secreting glandular epithelium
•

("palm folds"), also known as cervical crypts or
glands.
•
In the presence of high estrogen levels at ovulation,
the cells lining the crypts secrete abundant alkaline
secretions (grey arrows). This mucus lubricates the
vagina and nourishes spermatozoa.
•
Inflammation alters epithelial integrity → friability,
bleeding, ↑ susceptibility to ascending infection, and
progression to neoplasia (esp. HPV-related).

Vaginitis: Etiology, Pathogenesis, Pathophysiology
LO: Discuss common pelvic infections, including those affecting the vulva, vagina, and cervix, and describe the
pathogenesis of these diseases, common organisms involved, and related complications.
Etiology:
•
Infectious causes: Bacterial vaginosis (BV): overgrowth of Gardnerella vaginalis &
anaerobes after ↓ Lactobacillus.
•
Vulvovaginal candidiasis (VVC): Candida albicans overgrowth triggered by antibiotics,
hormones, immunosuppression.
•
Trichomoniasis: Trichomonas vaginalis (protozoan STI).
•
Noninfectious causes: atrophic vaginitis (low estrogen), allergic/chemical irritation, foreign
body, desquamative inflammatory vaginitis, lichen planus.
Pathogenesis:
•
BV: Loss of protective H₂O₂-producing Lactobacillus → ↑ anaerobes (e.g., Gardnerella).
•
VVC: Candida shifts from commensal yeast → pathogenic hyphae.
•
Trichomoniasis: direct protozoal infection.
•
Common mechanism: oxidative stress → ROS, cytokine release (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) →
epithelial injury & inflammation.
Pathophysiology & Clinical Features:
•
BV: Thin, gray-white, fishy discharge; ↑ pH > 4.5; clue cells on microscopy.
•
VVC: Thick, “cottage-cheese” discharge; erythema; yeast & pseudohyphae on KOH prep.
•
Trichomoniasis: Frothy yellow-green discharge, strawberry cervix, motile flagellates on
wet mount.
•
Atrophic vaginitis: Postmenopausal; dryness, burning, dyspareunia; thin mucosa, loss of
rugae, ↑ pH.

Vaginitis: Complications, Diagnosis, Treatment
LO: Discuss common pelvic infections, including those affecting the vulva, vagina, and cervix, and describe the pathogenesis of these diseases,
common organisms involved, and related complications.
Complications:
• ↑ Risk of pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), adverse pregnancy outcomes (preterm labor, low birth weight).
• Trichomoniasis increases susceptibility to HIV and other STIs.
• Chronic/recurrent infections affect quality of life.
Diagnosis
• BV: Amsel’s criteria (discharge, pH >4.5, clue cells, whiff test), Nugent score on Gram stain.
• VVC: KOH prep (budding yeast, pseudohyphae); culture for non-albicans species.
• Trichomoniasis: Wet mount (motile protozoa), NAAT.
• Atrophic: Clinical exam, microscopy (↑ WBCs, parabasal cells, ↓ Lactobacilli).
Treatment
•
BV: Metronidazole or clindamycin (oral or topical).
•
VVC: Oral fluconazole (single dose) or topical azoles; longer regimens for complicated/recurrent cases.
•
Trichomoniasis: Metronidazole/tinidazole (single dose); treat partners.
•
Atrophic vaginitis: Topical vaginal estrogen; lubricants/moisturizers.

Bartholin’s Glands:
LO: Correlate the histological structure of greater vestibular (Bartholin’s)
glands and paraurethral glands with their functions.
•
Location of the swelling: The mass is described as being at the
posterolateral vestibule near the labia minora.
•
This is the classic location of the Bartholin glands, which are
situated at the 5 and 7 o’clock positions of the vaginal introitus.
•
The mucinous glands seen here make up the Bartholin's gland in
the wall of the vagina.
•
This small gland is about 1 cm in size and the secretions drain
via a duct lined by cuboidal to transitional epithelium.
•
Function and duct obstruction: Bartholin glands secrete mucus
for vaginal lubrication, especially during sexual arousal.
•
Their ducts open into the vestibule, and duct obstruction can
lead to cyst formation (transitional epithelium) or abscess,
which presents as painful, fluctuant swelling.
•
Clinical presentation: Unilateral pain and swelling with a
fluctuant mass is typical of a Bartholin gland abscess.
•
Paraurethral gland (Skene) glands: infections are less common
and usually present with urethral symptoms (e.g., dysuria,
purulent discharge from the urethra), not posterolateral
vestibular swelling.

The squamocolumnar junction SCJ and the Transformation Zone
LO: Correlate the histological structure of the cervix with its function.
Predict functional outcomes that result from altered structure and function of the uterine cervix
(e.g., cervical cancer).
1.
Fetal stage
•
The SCJ (where squamous epithelium of the ectocervix meets columnar
epithelium of the endocervix) is located relatively low on the cervix.
•
During development, the columnar epithelium extends outward.
2. Pre-pubertal stage
•
The cervix is smaller, and the SCJ moves inward (up in the canal) compared to the
fetal stage.
•
This is because of cervical growth and changes in the hormonal environment (low
estrogen state before puberty).
3. Post-adolescent (eversion and transformation zone)
•
With puberty and estrogen stimulation, the cervix enlarges, and columnar
epithelium everts outward onto the ectocervix.
•
This exposed columnar epithelium undergoes squamous metaplasia, forming the
transformation zone (high-risk site for HPV infection and cervical neoplasia). The
area between the original SCJ and the newly formed SCJ is known as the
transformation Zone (TZ).
4. Post-menopausal stage
•
Due to low estrogen, the cervix shrinks, and the SCJ recedes further upward into
the endocervical canal.
•
The transformation zone becomes smaller and less accessible.

Pathogenesis of Cervical Intraepithelial Neoplasia (CINs)
LO: Discuss the common human papillomavirus types that affect the cervix and discuss the pathogenesis of cervical dysplasia and neoplasia, and
cervical screening methods and prevention.
•
CINs are the precursor lesions of cervical carcinomas.
•
Arise from normal cervical epithelium; low-grade and high-grade CINs.
•
The most important factor in the pathogenesis is the High-risk HPV (hrHPV).
•
Other contributing factors (smoking, immune status)
•
Low oncogenic risk HPV (types 6 and 11) and high oncogenic risk (types 16 and 18; longer to clear) based on their genotypes
•
High-risk HPVs are the main pathogenic factors of squamous cell carcinomas of other tissues (e.g., oropharynx, tonsils, anus,
vagina, vulva, and penis).
•
Risk factors associated with HPV infections are early age at first intercourse, multiple sexual partners, and persistent infection
with high-risk strains of HPV.
•
The hrHPV infection into the cervical epithelium results in host genome alterations, leading to the silencing of various tumor-
suppressor factors on one hand and inducing aberrant functioning of various tumor-promoting factors on the other
•
The imbalance and instability caused by various hrHPV-derived oncogenic factors drive neoplastic progression

## Page 36

Pathogenesis of Cervical Intraepithelial Neoplasia (CINs)-Cont
•
The major oncoproteins are E5, E6, and E7 encoded by the HPV genome
•
They disrupt the normal functioning of the major histocompatibility complex I (MHC class I), p53 and Rb, Notch1, Wnt,
MAPK, mTOR, and STAT-associated pathways, which are central players controlling normal cellular growth, differentiation,
and immune function
•
HPVs induce carcinogenesis through dysregulation of the activity of tumor suppressor proteins such as p53 and RB by the
viral E6 and E7 proteins, respectively---prevents the normal arrest of TZ cells in the G1 phase of the cell cycle.
•
The viral E6 upregulates telomerase and binds to p53, leading to its destruction.
•
E7 binds to the RB protein, leading to the release of the E2F transcription factors from the inhibitory effect of RB. This results
in progression through the cell cycle.
•
High-risk HPVs also integrate into the host cell genome. This integration results in increased expression of the viral E6 and E7
proteins, and host cell genomic instability----acquisition of prooncogenic mutations.
•
Low-risk HPVs do not integrate into the host genome.
•
The mechanism for low-grade dysplasia is accumulation of viral particles (DNA), producing the classic koilocyte
• Although CIN II/III has a higher risk for progression to invasive cervical carcinoma, not all women experience this; in fact, only
about 20% progress.
• VIN (vaginal intraepithelial neoplasia) is similar in appearance to CIN but affects the vagina and affects an older population. It
may represent a field effect from viral infection of the cervix.
